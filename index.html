Key Points

  • Former Deepmind researcher Thore Graepel founded Metis Reasoning to make AI systems more reliable and verifiable, using AlphaGo's approach as a blueprint.
  • The method separates knowledge from model weights and stores it in an external ledger, where an independent module checks every assumption against evidence.
  • The approach aims to cut hallucinations and provide source-backed explanations, but so far it exists only as a theoretical framework with no working system.

Former Deepmind researcher Thore Graepel wants to build AI systems that track what they know and check every conclusion against evidence. Larger language models won't fix this problem, his team says.

Thore Graepel believes the same idea that let AlphaGo beat Go professional Lee Sedol in 2016 can make AI systems more reliable, including in robots. The former Deepmind researcher founded Metis Reasoning and laid out the approach at Nvidia's GTC conference in Berlin, where the chipmaker has been expanding its platform for robotics and physical AI with the goal of turning robotics' data problem into a compute problem.

A knowledge ledger instead of knowledge baked into model weights

Today's language models amount to just AlphaGo's prediction network, which suggested possible moves, according to Metis. AlphaGo paired that network with a systematic search through game sequences and found the famous Move 37, a move the network would have given a human player roughly a one-in-ten-thousand chance of making. Metis wants to bring that kind of search to general reasoning.

Doing so requires what the company's paper calls an explicit knowledge ledger, comparable to the game board. Similar ideas already exist: Microsoft's GraphRAG organizes knowledge outside model weights in a knowledge graph, and Google's AlphaGeometry2 pairs a language model with a symbolic engine that verifies mathematical proofs. Metis wants to extend those principles to open questions in the real world.

Ad

Its knowledge ledger records what is known, how confident the system is, and which questions remain open. A separate module checks new entries against calculations, formal proofs, or empirical evidence, and when an assumption falls away, every conclusion that depended on it gets re-evaluated. A "move" can be a question, an experiment, or a challenge to an existing assumption; the system picks whichever one reduces uncertainty about the original question the most, ultimately delivering either an answer or a justified abstention.

As in AlphaGo, two neural networks suggest and evaluate moves, trained on logged prior investigations but unable to override the verification rules. Metis says the setup should produce fewer hallucinations, explanations with source citations, and knowledge that can be changed or deleted without retraining. Because reasoning breaks into small steps, the approach could also favor smaller models running on edge devices and in robots.

Companies still don't trust language models enough to deploy them

More data and compute improve language models but don't solve the technology's fundamental problems, according to Metis: the models fabricate answers, their reasoning chains can't be verified, and their knowledge is locked inside the weights. Hallucinations come from the training process itself, the paper argues, because models learn to produce plausible text rather than true text, and even the chains of thought in today's reasoning models often fail to show how an answer was actually reached.

That, the paper says, is why AI has had so little economic impact so far. When an agent acts on behalf of a company, errors are hard to trace back to their source and wrong knowledge can't be removed from the model. AI took off in software engineering because code is easy to test and roll back, but in medicine, finance, chip design, and robotics, the paper argues, AI needs to be verifiable from the ground up.

Ad

The white paper describes only a research program, with no working system, no benchmarks, and no comparisons with current reasoning models. The team acknowledges that the real world, unlike Go, provides only partial information, that evidence can be expensive to obtain, and that some questions simply have no answer. The biggest open question is how a verified knowledge ledger would take shape in messy domains beyond textbook examples, given that symbolic AI systems once failed precisely because all their knowledge had to be entered by hand.

After Silver and Rocktäschel, another Deepmind veteran strikes out on his own

Graepel earned his PhD in machine learning in Berlin in 2001, according to his website. At Microsoft Research he helped build TrueSkill, the matchmaking system behind Xbox Live. He then moved to Deepmind, where he worked on AlphaGo, AlphaZero, and MuZero. After a stint at Altos Labs he most recently served on Google Deepmind's post-AGI team, which he left over the summer. He also holds the machine learning chair at University College London. According to Bloomberg, Graepel is raising a low tens-of-millions round for Metis, with a follow-on of several hundred million dollars at a higher valuation already in the works.

He's the latest architect of Deepmind's best-known breakthroughs to build something outside Google. Former colleague David Silver founded Ineffable Intelligence and closed the largest seed round of any European startup. Tim Rocktäschel co-founded Recursive, now valued at $4.65 billion. Others are going straight to competitors. According to the Financial Times, Deepmind dissolved the team behind AlphaFold, and Nobel laureate John Jumper left for Anthropic with two colleagues. CNBC traces the talent drain partly to limited internal access to Google's TPU chips and bureaucracy, while Deepmind itself is losing its independence and founder Demis Hassabis has stepped back from day-to-day operations.

Global Event UK shop price inflation ‘highest since 2024’ as retailers struggle to limit price rises
🌍 Major Global Event • The Guardian Economics

British Retail Consortium says ‘dam is starting to break on the huge wave of costs’ being kept from consumers

Shop price inflation has risen to the highest level in two and a half years, according to the British Retail Consortium, which warned that “the dam is starting to break” when it comes to retailers trying to limit price rises for customers.

The lobby group said shop price inflation was 2.1% year on year in October, a sharp rise compared with 1.4% in September, and an indication that retailers were beginning to pass on the rising costs of energy and food ingredients since the Middle East conflict broke out.

It said food inflation, which has remained low during the past year, increased this month to 2.9% year on year – against a 2.5% rise in September – putting further pressure on the chancellor, John Healey, before his first budget on Wednesday.

Non-food inflation, which includes items such as clothing and footwear, more than doubled in October, from 0.8% to 1.7% year on year.

The price of Brent crude oil reached $117 a barrel in recent days, well above the $70 average in the months before the US and Israel attacked Iran in February. Gas prices have also risen sharply, pushing the UK consumer prices index to 3.5% last month.

Until this month, retailers had largely absorbed the higher costs of raw materials, mainly in response to weak demand from customers. In surveys, consumer confidence has declined sharply since the summer, when it became clearer the conflict in the Middle East was unlikely to end swiftly.

Bank of England officials are expected to raise interest rates next week from 3.75% to 4%. If the rise in retail prices persists, it is likely to persuade many of the Bank’s nine-member monetary policy committee to raise rates still further in December or the new year.

Helen Dickinson, the chief executive of the BRC, said: “The dam is starting to break on the huge wave of costs retailers have absorbed over the last two years.”

She said that while there was heavy discounting to attract shoppers ahead of Halloween celebrations, supermarkets could no longer keep the prices of tins and packets on their shelves from going up.

The chancellor is expected to come under intense pressure from his own backbenchers concerned that the high cost of living is harming many of their constituents.

The impact of cost of living pressures on low-income families is the subject of a report by Ruth Patrick, a professor in social and public policy at the University of Glasgow.

Based on a long-term study, it shows that high petrol and diesel prices are hitting families who need to transport disabled children to and from school and health appointments, and workers who use a car to travel to and from their jobs.

Petrol prices above £1.75 a litre in many places and diesel often above £2 a litre can mean that work doesn’t pay, according to Patrick, who leads Changing Realities, an online project working in partnership with over 200 hundred families.

She said that in the last month almost three-quarters (73%) of participants surveyed for the project had shopped in the reduced-items aisle, while 62% had changed the food they buy and 63% had left the heating off even when cold.

One respondent, Rosie L, who lives in rural Scotland, said: “It’s getting to a point where I don’t know how we are going to keep managing to run our car.” She added: “Public transport is not accessible or reliable where we are [and] not having access to a car would affect every single aspect of our lives. Particularly as my children are disabled.”

The report involves responses from 76 participants, of which 73 shared strategies they had taken to reduce costs over the previous month.

Patrick said the help provided by governments and local authorities so far did not match the scale of the problem. “The budget provides an opportunity to make good on Andy Burnham’s pledge that his will be a ‘cost of living government’,” she argued.

“Taking sustainable and long-term steps to secure the adequacy of social security would be a vital step here, and there is particular scope in restoring the real value of child benefit, traditionally a vital source of support with the additional costs that come with children.”

Indian Economy Telangana to hold all-party meet to discuss El Nino, drought
🇮🇳 Indian Economy • The Hindu BusinessLine

The Telangana Government will hold an all-party meeting on Wednesday to discuss the impact of El Nino, the rainfall deficit and drought conditions in the State.

Deputy Chief Minister Bhatti Vikramarka will chair the meeting, which will be held at the Dr BR Ambedkar Telangana State Secretariat here.

The meeting would discuss measures to reduce the impact of the weather conditions and the rainfall deficit on people and farmers.

The government would seek suggestions from political party leaders on drinking water supply, water resource conservation, power supply for irrigation, and an alternative crop plan.

It would also take their views on strategies to tackle the drought and the measures needed to mitigate the crisis.

Indian Policy What's the Meat of the Matter?
🏛️ Independent Policy & Society • The India Forum

Author: Sayan Deori, Tanya Chaudhary, Shobhit Mahajan, Rahul Goel, Sudha Narayanan, Shree Saha, Aditi Gautam, Thomas Reardon, S. Gunasekaran, Seema Mundoli, Somdatta Karak, Salik Ahmad

Food is extremely political in India, embroiled in the larger politics of religion and caste. Over the last decade, these controversies have been further heightened. Basic nutritional food like eggs for children in schools has been scrapped in several states. Eating one’s favourite mutton curry during Hindu festivals may not be possible as the state shuts down meat shops during that period. Public spaces are segregated based on diet preferences, like when a mess in IIT Bombay introduced 'pure veg’ dining table. Vigilante mobs have raided kitchens and fridges on suspicion of beef consumption, and state diplomatic banquets have turned into ‘pure vegetarian’ galas. These are not isolated incidents, but connected events engineered and enabled by the current majoritarian Hindutva regime.

Karpagam highlights the caste angle of vegetarian food in India, and how the vegetarian imposition is carried out in India by the state...

The Meat of the Matter: How the Politics of Food Shapes Our Health by Sylvia Karpagam sits at the intersection of nutrition science and the politics of meat and animal source foods that is being played out in contemporary India. She enters the debate on the political and nutritional economy of food and the politics of idolising normative diets. Karpagam is a medical doctor and has faced online trolling for her views on food and nutrition, something she mentions in the book as well—these include casteist slurs like ‘rice bag convert’. Her book picks up questions related to food politics in contemporary India, and elaborates on them through a synthesis of evidence and data from published studies related to nutrition and public health.

The narrative thread of the book is the fictitious story of Princess Amudam of Arogya Nadu, a prosperous kingdom high on the Human Development Index, journeying to Starva Pradesh, a kingdom performing poorly on every variable of human development and public health. Along the princess’s passage between the kingdoms, Karapagam weaves in her account of contemporary Indian malnutrition and food issues. This story is threaded into all the chapters. The book can be broadly categorised into three categories for the readers: human nutrition, the burden of malnutrition, and the vegetarian and meat discourse that shapes policies.

The first three chapters provide a clinical register of human nutrition from the role of various macro- and micro-nutrients like proteins, fats, minerals and vitamins. They are textbook styled, outlining the scientific understanding of nutrients, their sources, and their bodily benefits, through fact-boxes of facts and blobs of information in sections like “Did You Know?” and “Debunking Myths on the Plate”. The end of each chapter has bulleted “Key Takeaways”. While it felt slightly stretchy at parts, the synthesis of nutritional science over many decades builds a coherent narrative on nutrition.

From nutrition science, Karpagam moves onto malnutrition, drawing on population level data and studies across time periods and regions. She illustrates the high burden of undernutrition in India and its indicators like stunting, wasting, and underweight, through the National Family Health Surveys (NFHS) and the Comprehensive National Nutrition Survey (CNNS 2019). She also discusses the high prevalence of nutritional anaemia, mostly caused due to iron deficiency, to which women of reproductive age groups are the most vulnerable. Through these chapters, Karpagam also busts fads popular on social media, including 'Soya is Super Food', and  'Millets are Magical', through a careful explanation of nutrition bioavailability—how much of the theoretical nutrition of the food item the body can actually absorb—and dietary guidelines.

The third engagement of the book is with the politics of food and its manifestation in everyday life. Karpagam highlights the caste angle of vegetarian food in India, and how the vegetarian imposition is carried out in India by the state through ideological interference in public nutritional programmes. She highlights how concerns over ‘purity’ have caused eggs to be removed from midday meals in schools in several states.

In particular, she critiques the Akshya Patra Foundation (APF), whose collaboration with state governments in supplying school lunches promotes ‘sattvic food’. She mentions that “the foundation has a religious prerogative of advocating a de facto vegeterian diet, strictly avoiding meat, fish and eggs, and considers onions and garlic in food as lower modes of nature which inhibits spiritual advancement”. Not only this, but she also flags the APF’s financial opacity and its strong caste linkages and state patronages that it receives. These kinds of organisations are complicit in advancing and making a Hindutva state.

Karpagam challenges the narrative of the vegetarian discourse of nutrition by juxtaposing it with majoritarianism.

Karpagam also sheds light on the EAT-Lancet 2019 report and the “sustainable diet for planetary health” agenda that was being circulated and also platformed and promoted by India's food safety regulator. She touches upon the big food corporate nexus in nutrition policies. The EAT Lancet report 2019 propagated a global narrative to shift from a meat-based diet to a plant-based diet, and particularly idolised India as an ideal vegetarian society. She criticises the report's prescriptions: a clamp down on animal source foods, equating them as being as unhealthy as sugar, a hint at moving towards a plant based diet, and looking for “alternative proteins”. She mentions big corporations are privy to this and have appropriated the term “plant based”. Through reports like these, they manufacture new knowledge and consensus in creating systems that brings them more profits under the garb of science.

The Meat of the Matter provides a scientific understanding of major food and public health nutrition related controversies in India. Karpagam challenges the narrative of the vegetarian discourse of nutrition by juxtaposing it with majoritarianism. While she does not mention her methodology, or how she has collected her data, or selected her themes, this book can be read as a synthesis of the several structural harms that have been triggered by food and food cultures. I felt a deeper reading of the history of caste and violence in food politics and a critical perspective on policies that have led to our current nutritional systems could have been included. For instance, why do certain nutrients and foods become ‘heroes’ in certain decades or junctures. These include the rise of protein in the 1950s to the 70s, the micronutrients turn in the 90s, and later the emergence of ‘smarter foods’ like bio-fortification and fortification by nutrients. These events have shaped nutrition policies globally.

Although she mentions and acknowledges situated traditional knowledge of community in nourishment practices, we do not find in Karpagam's narrative a reference to the debates over how a biomedical approach to malnutrition can be a problematic hegemony, especially for countries in the Global South, where land dispossession, colonisation, caste location and socio economic factors shape nutrition. Yet, the accessible language used to present the science makes The Meat of the Matter an important book for people to understand food and nutrition politics in India.

Sayan Deori is a PhD scholar at Tezpur University, Assam.
