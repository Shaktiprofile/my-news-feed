AI & Tech Fancy harnesses make coding agents more expensive but not better, study finds

Makers tout score gains from new agent harnesses like Claude Code or OpenCode. A study finds that claim doesn't hold up. Swapping harnesses barely moves results more than running the same setup twice, but costs can triple.

A coding agent pairs a language model with a harness, the system prompt, tools, and context management that make the model work inside a code repository. New harnesses ship every day, and leaderboards mix them freely, but two researchers at Shandong University measured how much the harness actually matters when you hold the model constant. The paper's title gives away the answer: "What does a harness buy? Mostly tokens."

Earlier comparisons were measuring noise

Previous studies report gaps of more than 20 points between harnesses, but those numbers almost always come from a single run, according to the authors. Pure reruns of the same setup already vary by up to six points on SWE-bench Verified. Zhipu AI, for example, advertises its own harness with a 2.4-point lead over Claude Code, a margin that falls well within that noise range. Harnesses are also often wired into the benchmark differently, making comparisons even less reliable.

Liu and Han ran five models through three harnesses in their shipped versions: Claude Code, OpenCode, and mini-SWE-agent. mini-SWE-agent gives the model only a Bash tool and skips context management. Claude Code and OpenCode offer specialized file and search tools, long system prompts, and automatic history compression.

The models were Qwen3.6, Qwen3.8, Deepseek-V4-Flash, GLM-5.3-Flash, and HY4-Preview. Claude Opus 5 ran as a reference only in Claude Code. Tests used the 45 hardest tasks from SWE-bench Verified plus a pool of 447 more. Several setups ran twice to measure noise.

All runs happened offline. In early tests with web access, 71 percent of Deepseek and Qwen model attempts pulled the correct fix straight from GitHub instead of solving the task themselves. Claude models almost never did this. The researchers discarded those results and don't claim any maker trained on the test data, but the pattern was strong enough that they cut off web access for all remaining runs.

Swapping harnesses moves results about as much as a rerun

On the larger pool of 447 tasks, Claude Code and mini-SWE-agent, the heaviest and lightest harnesses, sit less than two points apart for both tested models. Within a five-point band, they're statistically equivalent.

The researchers expected the biggest harness effect on the 45 hardest tasks. Instead, harnesses differ by two to five solved tasks per model, and a simple rerun of the same setup shifts the count by up to three. Sometimes mini-SWE-agent leads, sometimes Claude Code does.

The flip rates tell the same story. Between two reruns, the median outcome changes on 13 percent of tasks. Between two different harnesses, it's also 13 percent. Swapping models pushes that to 22 percent. Which tasks a given harness wins changes from run to run, matching a coin flip. The harness gap behaves like a "fresh draw of the same noise," the authors write.

These small samples also limit what any comparison can detect. With 45 tasks, even a 13-point gap shows up only half the time. With 447 tasks, gaps of about five points become reliably detectable. Zhipu's claimed 2.4-point advantage falls below both thresholds, and so do many other advertised improvements.

All harnesses perform the same, except OpenCode falls behind

The only measurable harness effect is a steady deficit for OpenCode. On the large task pool, it trails the other two by up to nine points. About half of that gap traces back to an output limit. When a model exceeds this limit mid-step during reasoning, Claude Code prompts it to keep going. OpenCode lets the step end there, and the attempt usually fails.

Adding a Claude Code-style continuation prompt to OpenCode didn't help, because the same task rarely triggers the same overflow on a retry. For the rest of the gap, the authors have no explanation, only an observation: under OpenCode, models stop working much sooner. On locally run models, OpenCode also had a much tighter time limit than Claude Code, which the authors flag as an uncontrolled variable.

mini-SWE-agent runs into the context window because it lacks context management, mostly on tasks where the other harnesses also fail. OpenCode's search tools try to download a binary file on first use and break when offline.

System prompt and tool descriptions set the bill

With the same model, same tasks, and same price list, a single task can cost up to three times more depending on the harness. Tool outputs barely explain the gap. All three harnesses return similar amounts of text per call.

The real difference comes from the system prompt and tool descriptions. They get resent with every step and run about 16,600 tokens for Claude Code, roughly 7,000 for OpenCode, and under 1,000 for mini-SWE-agent. Claude Code also keeps the model's reasoning output in the conversation history, based on the researchers' reverse engineering.

After that first call, context grows at similar rates across all harnesses. The system prompt size sets the baseline cost, and the number of steps multiplies it. Which factor dominates depends on the model: With GLM-5.3-Flash, Claude Code costs nearly twice as much as mini-SWE-agent. With Deepseek-V4-Flash, mini-SWE-agent needs so many extra steps that the two end up even. Lower prices for cached tokens dampen the gaps but don't change the ranking.

No home-field advantage, open questions around thinking

Deepseek's own harness scores no better with its own model than the other harnesses do, but costs the most. Claude Opus 5 delivered identical results even after the researchers removed tools from Claude Code. A year-old version solves a similar number of tasks at less than half the cost, because Extended Thinking wasn't on by default back then.

Thinking was the only case where the harness choice actually changed the outcome. Without thinking, mini-SWE-agent with Qwen3.6 drops eleven points, while Claude Code and OpenCode stay within noise. HY4-Preview also loses eleven points in Claude Code without thinking. How much an agent depends on reasoning seems tied to the specific model-harness combination. The authors admit their test grid is too small to explain why.

The study relies mainly on one benchmark. A small cross-check on Terminal-Bench 2.1 shows the same pattern. The closed-source top model, Opus 5, ran only in Claude Code. A harness buys " fewer ways to lose a task, and a bill, not pass rate," the authors conclude. They recommend publishing a rerun of the same setup alongside every harness comparison. That's the only way to tell how much of a measured difference is just noise. Data and analysis scripts are available on GitHub.

More effort in the harness rarely pays off in other studies, either. In one, a simple Markdown file beat a complex skill system for AI coding agents. An earlier study already showed that the choice of framework can nearly triple costs for the same model. Harness design gets a lot of attention as a result. Nvidia recently cut nearly half its token costs for coding agents through better agent steering.

Global Event UK consumer confidence falls at fastest rate since Brexit vote as cost of living bites
🌍 Major Global Event • The Guardian Economics

Economic fallout from war in Middle East adds to pressures on consumers and on chancellor before budget

Consumer confidence in the UK has fallen at the fastest rate outside the Covid pandemic since the 2016 Brexit vote as the escalating economic damage from the war in the Middle East hits Britain.

With less than a week before John Healey’s first budget, the latest barometer from the data company GfK indicated confidence plunged by 11 percentage points in October to -24 amid pressure on consumers from the rising cost of living.

Highlighting the challenge for Andy Burnham and his chancellor before the statement on Wednesday, GfK said its consumer confidence index last fell by this much in a single month in July 2016, following the shock leave result in the EU referendum.

The index – which has been closely tracked by the government and the Bank of England for early warning signs from the economy since the early 1970s – recorded its largest ever fall, of 25 points, in March 2020 just after the start of the first UK lockdown.

The latest snapshot represents a marked change from the summer, when GfK said a “Burnham bounce”, along with a strong World Cup run by the men’s England football team and sunny weather, lifted the mood of consumers in its long-running monthly survey.

Neil Bellamy, consumer insights director at GfK, said: “Relentless cost-of-living pressures, against an international backdrop of unresolved regional conflicts that are themselves contributing to domestic inflation now at 3.5%, appear to have swept aside the nascent optimism we saw following the appointment of a new UK prime minister this summer.”

It comes as consumers face a fresh squeeze on living standards triggered by the unfolding war in the Middle East. Petrol and diesel prices have surged close to record highs and energy bills have increased in advance of a difficult winter for households already feeling the pinch from years of price increases.

Over the past five years analysis by the Trades Union Congress shows that UK households have paid almost £14,000 on average in additional costs for mortgages, loans, energy and water bills.

With less than a week before his tax and spending speech to the Commons, the chancellor is under pressure to take action to support households amid warnings that consumer energy bills could reach £2,000 a year from January for the typical household.

Healey has pledged to use his statement to announce “breathing space” measures to ease the pressure. However, he has also warned that the government has limited resources to intervene as the Middle East conflict pushes the UK’s borrowing costs to the highest level in decades.

Official figures released on Wednesday show headline inflation rose by more than expected to 3.5% last month, while the government deficit was a worse-than-forecast £24.5bn.

In a double blow for the chancellor, the twin pieces of bad news underscore the fact that consumers are facing a renewed cost of living squeeze, just as Healey is also under increased financial pressure.

According to the latest figures from GfK, consumers became less confident about the strength of their own personal finances, as well as the general outlook for the British economy at large.

The major purchase index fell by 14 points to -22 in October, suggesting that consumers could be bracing to cut back on spending over the winter months to make room in their finances for higher energy bills.

Separate figures from the CBI show investment intentions among manufacturing businesses were subdued as cost pressures remained high in the three months to October.

Manufacturing output is expected to stabilise in the next three months, according to the lobby group’s latest Industrial Trends Survey. However, cost pressures for businesses remained elevated.

Cameron Martin, senior economist at the CBI, said: “If the government wants to meaningfully tackle the cost of living, it must give manufacturers the breathing space to invest and drive the productivity growth needed to sustain rising real wages.

“Next week’s budget must deliver this by cutting business electricity costs, reducing labour costs and reforming business rates to support rather than hinder investment and growth.”

The Treasury has been approached for comment.

Indian Economy Air India and AI Express cancel Riyadh, Dammam flights till Nov 7
🇮🇳 Indian Economy • The Hindu BusinessLine

Air India and Air India Express on Saturday extended the cancellation of their flights connecting Riyadh and Dammam till November 7, while services to Jeddah will remain suspended till October 31.

IndiGo and Akasa Air have also cancelled their services to Saudi Arabian cities amid escalating geopolitical tensions in the region and attacks at airports in the Gulf country.

"In view of the prevailing situation in the region, Air India and Air India Express flights to and from Riyadh and Dammam will remain suspended until 7 November 2026, while services to and from Jeddah will remain suspended until 31 October 2026," Air India said in a post on X.

The airline said passengers holding confirmed bookings issued on or before October 24 may avail flexible rebooking options or opt for a full refund upon cancellation of their bookings.

Earlier, the two airlines cancelled their services to Saudi Arabia till October 24.

IndiGo on Saturday said that after a careful assessment, flights to and from Jeddah, Dammam and Madinah will also remain cancelled till October 31.

On Friday, the airline announced cancelling services to Riyadh till October 31, while for the other three cities, the cancellation of flights was till October 27.

Akasa Air, on Friday, said it extended the suspension of flights connecting Riyadh till November 30 and to Jeddah till November 10.

Indian Policy What's the Meat of the Matter?
🏛️ Independent Policy & Society • The India Forum

Author: Sayan Deori, Tanya Chaudhary, Shobhit Mahajan, Rahul Goel, Sudha Narayanan, Shree Saha, Aditi Gautam, Thomas Reardon, S. Gunasekaran, Seema Mundoli, Somdatta Karak, Salik Ahmad

Food is extremely political in India, embroiled in the larger politics of religion and caste. Over the last decade, these controversies have been further heightened. Basic nutritional food like eggs for children in schools has been scrapped in several states. Eating one’s favourite mutton curry during Hindu festivals may not be possible as the state shuts down meat shops during that period. Public spaces are segregated based on diet preferences, like when a mess in IIT Bombay introduced 'pure veg’ dining table. Vigilante mobs have raided kitchens and fridges on suspicion of beef consumption, and state diplomatic banquets have turned into ‘pure vegetarian’ galas. These are not isolated incidents, but connected events engineered and enabled by the current majoritarian Hindutva regime.

Karpagam highlights the caste angle of vegetarian food in India, and how the vegetarian imposition is carried out in India by the state...

The Meat of the Matter: How the Politics of Food Shapes Our Health by Sylvia Karpagam sits at the intersection of nutrition science and the politics of meat and animal source foods that is being played out in contemporary India. She enters the debate on the political and nutritional economy of food and the politics of idolising normative diets. Karpagam is a medical doctor and has faced online trolling for her views on food and nutrition, something she mentions in the book as well—these include casteist slurs like ‘rice bag convert’. Her book picks up questions related to food politics in contemporary India, and elaborates on them through a synthesis of evidence and data from published studies related to nutrition and public health.

The narrative thread of the book is the fictitious story of Princess Amudam of Arogya Nadu, a prosperous kingdom high on the Human Development Index, journeying to Starva Pradesh, a kingdom performing poorly on every variable of human development and public health. Along the princess’s passage between the kingdoms, Karapagam weaves in her account of contemporary Indian malnutrition and food issues. This story is threaded into all the chapters. The book can be broadly categorised into three categories for the readers: human nutrition, the burden of malnutrition, and the vegetarian and meat discourse that shapes policies.

The first three chapters provide a clinical register of human nutrition from the role of various macro- and micro-nutrients like proteins, fats, minerals and vitamins. They are textbook styled, outlining the scientific understanding of nutrients, their sources, and their bodily benefits, through fact-boxes of facts and blobs of information in sections like “Did You Know?” and “Debunking Myths on the Plate”. The end of each chapter has bulleted “Key Takeaways”. While it felt slightly stretchy at parts, the synthesis of nutritional science over many decades builds a coherent narrative on nutrition.

From nutrition science, Karpagam moves onto malnutrition, drawing on population level data and studies across time periods and regions. She illustrates the high burden of undernutrition in India and its indicators like stunting, wasting, and underweight, through the National Family Health Surveys (NFHS) and the Comprehensive National Nutrition Survey (CNNS 2019). She also discusses the high prevalence of nutritional anaemia, mostly caused due to iron deficiency, to which women of reproductive age groups are the most vulnerable. Through these chapters, Karpagam also busts fads popular on social media, including 'Soya is Super Food', and  'Millets are Magical', through a careful explanation of nutrition bioavailability—how much of the theoretical nutrition of the food item the body can actually absorb—and dietary guidelines.

The third engagement of the book is with the politics of food and its manifestation in everyday life. Karpagam highlights the caste angle of vegetarian food in India, and how the vegetarian imposition is carried out in India by the state through ideological interference in public nutritional programmes. She highlights how concerns over ‘purity’ have caused eggs to be removed from midday meals in schools in several states.

In particular, she critiques the Akshya Patra Foundation (APF), whose collaboration with state governments in supplying school lunches promotes ‘sattvic food’. She mentions that “the foundation has a religious prerogative of advocating a de facto vegeterian diet, strictly avoiding meat, fish and eggs, and considers onions and garlic in food as lower modes of nature which inhibits spiritual advancement”. Not only this, but she also flags the APF’s financial opacity and its strong caste linkages and state patronages that it receives. These kinds of organisations are complicit in advancing and making a Hindutva state.

Karpagam challenges the narrative of the vegetarian discourse of nutrition by juxtaposing it with majoritarianism.

Karpagam also sheds light on the EAT-Lancet 2019 report and the “sustainable diet for planetary health” agenda that was being circulated and also platformed and promoted by India's food safety regulator. She touches upon the big food corporate nexus in nutrition policies. The EAT Lancet report 2019 propagated a global narrative to shift from a meat-based diet to a plant-based diet, and particularly idolised India as an ideal vegetarian society. She criticises the report's prescriptions: a clamp down on animal source foods, equating them as being as unhealthy as sugar, a hint at moving towards a plant based diet, and looking for “alternative proteins”. She mentions big corporations are privy to this and have appropriated the term “plant based”. Through reports like these, they manufacture new knowledge and consensus in creating systems that brings them more profits under the garb of science.

The Meat of the Matter provides a scientific understanding of major food and public health nutrition related controversies in India. Karpagam challenges the narrative of the vegetarian discourse of nutrition by juxtaposing it with majoritarianism. While she does not mention her methodology, or how she has collected her data, or selected her themes, this book can be read as a synthesis of the several structural harms that have been triggered by food and food cultures. I felt a deeper reading of the history of caste and violence in food politics and a critical perspective on policies that have led to our current nutritional systems could have been included. For instance, why do certain nutrients and foods become ‘heroes’ in certain decades or junctures. These include the rise of protein in the 1950s to the 70s, the micronutrients turn in the 90s, and later the emergence of ‘smarter foods’ like bio-fortification and fortification by nutrients. These events have shaped nutrition policies globally.

Although she mentions and acknowledges situated traditional knowledge of community in nourishment practices, we do not find in Karpagam's narrative a reference to the debates over how a biomedical approach to malnutrition can be a problematic hegemony, especially for countries in the Global South, where land dispossession, colonisation, caste location and socio economic factors shape nutrition. Yet, the accessible language used to present the science makes The Meat of the Matter an important book for people to understand food and nutrition politics in India.

Sayan Deori is a PhD scholar at Tezpur University, Assam.
