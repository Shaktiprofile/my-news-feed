Key Points

  • Anthropic filed its S-1 for a Nasdaq listing under the ticker "ANT."
  • In Q2 2026, Anthropic pulled in $11.6 billion in revenue and posted its first GAAP operating profit at $974 million. The flip side: long-term compute commitments totaling $780 to $810 billion.
  • Eighty percent of revenue comes from per-token billing based on actual usage. Existing customers are spending nine times more year over year on average.

Anthropic filed its IPO prospectus, and the market seems to like it.

The AI lab is the first major company of its kind to publicly file an S-1 with the SEC. Anthropic plans to list on the Nasdaq under the ticker "ANT," with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley leading the offering. Wall Street expects a valuation of roughly $2 trillion, according to Yahoo Finance, up from the company's most recent private valuation of about $965 billion, which already put it ahead of rival OpenAI at roughly $852 billion.

The first-half numbers show huge growth. Revenue jumped from $376 million in Q1 2025 to $11.6 billion in Q2 2026, a 1,368 percent year-over-year increase. For the first half of 2026, Anthropic brought in $16.3 billion total, more than triple its full-year 2025 revenue of $4.6 billion. Government contracts account for only about one percent of that total, while roughly 5 to 7 percent comes from customers who violate usage policies, primarily through model distillation, which Anthropic has already been cracking down on.

In Q2 2026, Anthropic posted its first positive GAAP operating profit at $974 million. Compute costs rose 300 percent over the same period, but that's slower than revenue growth. Revenue per employee sits at $6.6 million, higher than any Magnificent 7 company except Nvidia, with about 5,300 people on staff. Chip stocks like Nvidia, Broadcom, and AMD rallied after the filing went public.

Ad

Existing customers spend nine times more year over year

Anthropic's net revenue retention rate sits at roughly 900 percent, meaning existing customers are spending nine times what they did a year ago. That figure speaks to AI's scaling potential, especially considering most deployments are likely still shallow, depend on human oversight, and don't run AI at full speed yet.

More than 11,900 customers pay over $100,000 annually, and about 250 spend more than $10 million. No single customer accounts for more than ten percent of revenue, but five customers together make up 25 percent. If those include companies like Meta, Google, or Microsoft that could switch to their own or cheaper models, that concentration could become a risk.

Ad

Claude Enterprise users who also use Claude Code are the biggest spenders, paying about 20 times more than other customers, according to the S-1. Eighty percent of revenue is billed on actual usage, per token processed as the smallest billing unit for AI models, while the remaining 20 percent comes from fixed subscriptions. That split might signal how heavily subscriptions are still being cross-subsidized, but it also suggests Anthropic has room to raise prices. The company's forecast for next year will be primarily usage-based, according to the FT.

Compute commitments top $780 billion

Anthropic has taken on huge financial obligations to fuel its growth. Compute capacity contracts with Broadcom, Google, Amazon, Microsoft, and other providers add up to roughly $780 to $810 billion, and those costs are more or less fixed and locked in long-term, while revenue remains variable and usage-dependent.

Ad

That means the company needs to keep growing fast to cover its future bills. If it can't, the AI-fueled capital market could start to wobble. To gain more flexibility and lower costs over time, Anthropic is building its own data centers under the name "Anthropic Direct".

Ad

Anthropic also left Q3 numbers out of the S-1, even though the company already has them. One possible reason is that growth slowed significantly in Q3. Momentum may have already picked back up, though. OpenRouter data shows demand for Anthropic models climbed again after the October release of the 5.5 series, especially Opus 5.5. OpenRouter only captures a tiny slice of total token demand and tends to skew toward open-weight models, so if Anthropic is dominating there, it's likely doing even better everywhere else.

Global Event What the claw arcade game tells us about the cost of living crisis | Zoe Williams
🌍 Major Global Event • The Guardian Economics
Zoe Williams

No matter how you crunch the UK data, prices are rising while average wages stagnate. The system is rigged – and it’s time we kicked over the toy dispenser

About a decade ago, the financial crash was almost a decade old. Unrelatedly, my kids were under 10, so I was spending a lot more time in arcades. There’s a particular game involving a metal claw – it goes by the name of claw-game. You put in a pound, and try to manipulate the claw to grab a toy that is worth nothing like a pound. You would take that as the price of a bit of family fun, except the claw never grabbed a toy.

The frustration for a six-year-old was immense, every lost pound making the probably flammable stuffed hamster more urgently desirable. But it was way worse for me, because I was clinging on to some residual 80s idea of arcade-honour. Surely, after pound #7, the machine would just give you a sodding toy? That would represent an astronomical return, lessen the possibility of an adult kicking the machine to death and assure a returning market of maybe 10% of the dumbest or most spineless parents. But no, nada. No toys, ever.

So it wasn’t a good game, but it was starting to feel like a really good metaphor for the economy. This was in the twilight period between 2015 and Brexit, after which politics stopped troubling itself with day-to-day liveability. Previously, if you studied hard you got a job, then if you worked hard, you could afford to live; and in the fullness of time, if you met another person who worked hard, you could have a family and afford to feed them – this was basically the honour-code of capitalism. But it was breaking down and nobody wanted to admit it.

Trade unions noticed: the TUC crunched the numbers in 2014 and found that only one in 40 new jobs were full time. The geographer Danny Dorling noticed that a fifth of people needed a cash benefit to make rent, and average house prices were nearly nine times average salaries in England and Wales. The NHS noticed, in the same year, inasmuch as the number of children with vitamin D deficiency and rickets had tripled since 2009. A lot of people privately thought, “This whole thing is rigged,” though, as far as I know, I alone noticed the irresistible likeness to the Ramsgate arcade.

Scroll forward a decade and people are much more public about their noticing – but expectations have changed and with them, the language. We talk about a cost of living crisis, but we don’t cast ourselves as agents in the economy, whose wages aren’t keeping pace with prices. Rather, we’re a bunch of luckless dopes to whom bad things keep happening. The New Economics Foundation thinktank, with Best for Britain, has dropped into these new times an “attainability index”. It takes the wage data from the past 20 years and calculates how many hours you would have to work to attain a set of things: a holiday, a night out, an outfit, a car.

The figures aren’t at all surprising. Low-paid workers have their wages mostly eaten up by essentials. Social participation is sporadic, holidays out of reach. A secondary school teacher looks as though they are doing OK, because they would only have to work 23 hours to afford a ticket to a festival. But that would assume they are not going to do any other discretionary spending whatsoever.

Just about the only things that have gone down in price in real terms, over the past 20 years, are gym memberships, budget clothes and meal deals, which makes sense. In all the crazy supermarket moments of this decade, the remarkable value of a prawn mayo sandwich, as long as you get some crisps and a Red Bull at the same time, has often felt like the only thing standing between us and revolution.

The important thing isn’t the numbers (no offence, researchers, your data is beautiful) but the fact that everyone is experiencing the same degradation. The pilot of 2026 is as far removed in his or her housing prospects from the pilot of 2006, as is the chef or the accountant from theirs. So much political rhetoric goes into the creation of rivalrous silos – the woke v the anti-woke, the liberal elite v the left-behind – all of it constructed to mask the fact that everybody’s expectations have been thwarted for two decades straight. Realising this might shunt the discourse out of phase “get mad” into phase “get even”.

Zoe Williams is a Guardian columnist

Indian Economy Cyclone Arnab: Affected people in AP can report emergencies via Mana Mitra WhatsApp service
🇮🇳 Indian Economy • The Hindu BusinessLine

The Real Time Governance Society (RTGS), Andhra Pradesh has asked people in Cyclone Arnab-affected areas of State to report emergencies and local problems through Mana Mitra WhatsApp Governance.

People can report power outages, fallen trees, collapsed walls, waterlogging and other cyclone-related issues by sending “Hi” to 9552300009 on WhatsApp.

To register a complaint, citizens must select “Arnab Cyclone Issues”, followed by “Report Issue” and “Confirm”. They can then select “Registration”, enter their name, district and mandal, describe the problem and upload photographs, if available.

Addressing Grievances

Registered grievances will be forwarded to the concerned local authorities for necessary action and early resolution.

RTGS has appealed to citizens to use the facility to report cyclone-related problems promptly and help authorities respond effectively.

Once registered, the grievance will be forwarded to the concerned local authorities for necessary action and early resolution, a release said.

Indian Policy What's the Meat of the Matter?
🏛️ Independent Policy & Society • The India Forum

Author: Sayan Deori, Tanya Chaudhary, Shobhit Mahajan, Rahul Goel, Sudha Narayanan, Shree Saha, Aditi Gautam, Thomas Reardon, S. Gunasekaran, Seema Mundoli, Somdatta Karak, Salik Ahmad

Food is extremely political in India, embroiled in the larger politics of religion and caste. Over the last decade, these controversies have been further heightened. Basic nutritional food like eggs for children in schools has been scrapped in several states. Eating one’s favourite mutton curry during Hindu festivals may not be possible as the state shuts down meat shops during that period. Public spaces are segregated based on diet preferences, like when a mess in IIT Bombay introduced 'pure veg’ dining table. Vigilante mobs have raided kitchens and fridges on suspicion of beef consumption, and state diplomatic banquets have turned into ‘pure vegetarian’ galas. These are not isolated incidents, but connected events engineered and enabled by the current majoritarian Hindutva regime.

Karpagam highlights the caste angle of vegetarian food in India, and how the vegetarian imposition is carried out in India by the state...

The Meat of the Matter: How the Politics of Food Shapes Our Health by Sylvia Karpagam sits at the intersection of nutrition science and the politics of meat and animal source foods that is being played out in contemporary India. She enters the debate on the political and nutritional economy of food and the politics of idolising normative diets. Karpagam is a medical doctor and has faced online trolling for her views on food and nutrition, something she mentions in the book as well—these include casteist slurs like ‘rice bag convert’. Her book picks up questions related to food politics in contemporary India, and elaborates on them through a synthesis of evidence and data from published studies related to nutrition and public health.

The narrative thread of the book is the fictitious story of Princess Amudam of Arogya Nadu, a prosperous kingdom high on the Human Development Index, journeying to Starva Pradesh, a kingdom performing poorly on every variable of human development and public health. Along the princess’s passage between the kingdoms, Karapagam weaves in her account of contemporary Indian malnutrition and food issues. This story is threaded into all the chapters. The book can be broadly categorised into three categories for the readers: human nutrition, the burden of malnutrition, and the vegetarian and meat discourse that shapes policies.

The first three chapters provide a clinical register of human nutrition from the role of various macro- and micro-nutrients like proteins, fats, minerals and vitamins. They are textbook styled, outlining the scientific understanding of nutrients, their sources, and their bodily benefits, through fact-boxes of facts and blobs of information in sections like “Did You Know?” and “Debunking Myths on the Plate”. The end of each chapter has bulleted “Key Takeaways”. While it felt slightly stretchy at parts, the synthesis of nutritional science over many decades builds a coherent narrative on nutrition.

From nutrition science, Karpagam moves onto malnutrition, drawing on population level data and studies across time periods and regions. She illustrates the high burden of undernutrition in India and its indicators like stunting, wasting, and underweight, through the National Family Health Surveys (NFHS) and the Comprehensive National Nutrition Survey (CNNS 2019). She also discusses the high prevalence of nutritional anaemia, mostly caused due to iron deficiency, to which women of reproductive age groups are the most vulnerable. Through these chapters, Karpagam also busts fads popular on social media, including 'Soya is Super Food', and  'Millets are Magical', through a careful explanation of nutrition bioavailability—how much of the theoretical nutrition of the food item the body can actually absorb—and dietary guidelines.

The third engagement of the book is with the politics of food and its manifestation in everyday life. Karpagam highlights the caste angle of vegetarian food in India, and how the vegetarian imposition is carried out in India by the state through ideological interference in public nutritional programmes. She highlights how concerns over ‘purity’ have caused eggs to be removed from midday meals in schools in several states.

In particular, she critiques the Akshya Patra Foundation (APF), whose collaboration with state governments in supplying school lunches promotes ‘sattvic food’. She mentions that “the foundation has a religious prerogative of advocating a de facto vegeterian diet, strictly avoiding meat, fish and eggs, and considers onions and garlic in food as lower modes of nature which inhibits spiritual advancement”. Not only this, but she also flags the APF’s financial opacity and its strong caste linkages and state patronages that it receives. These kinds of organisations are complicit in advancing and making a Hindutva state.

Karpagam challenges the narrative of the vegetarian discourse of nutrition by juxtaposing it with majoritarianism.

Karpagam also sheds light on the EAT-Lancet 2019 report and the “sustainable diet for planetary health” agenda that was being circulated and also platformed and promoted by India's food safety regulator. She touches upon the big food corporate nexus in nutrition policies. The EAT Lancet report 2019 propagated a global narrative to shift from a meat-based diet to a plant-based diet, and particularly idolised India as an ideal vegetarian society. She criticises the report's prescriptions: a clamp down on animal source foods, equating them as being as unhealthy as sugar, a hint at moving towards a plant based diet, and looking for “alternative proteins”. She mentions big corporations are privy to this and have appropriated the term “plant based”. Through reports like these, they manufacture new knowledge and consensus in creating systems that brings them more profits under the garb of science.

The Meat of the Matter provides a scientific understanding of major food and public health nutrition related controversies in India. Karpagam challenges the narrative of the vegetarian discourse of nutrition by juxtaposing it with majoritarianism. While she does not mention her methodology, or how she has collected her data, or selected her themes, this book can be read as a synthesis of the several structural harms that have been triggered by food and food cultures. I felt a deeper reading of the history of caste and violence in food politics and a critical perspective on policies that have led to our current nutritional systems could have been included. For instance, why do certain nutrients and foods become ‘heroes’ in certain decades or junctures. These include the rise of protein in the 1950s to the 70s, the micronutrients turn in the 90s, and later the emergence of ‘smarter foods’ like bio-fortification and fortification by nutrients. These events have shaped nutrition policies globally.

Although she mentions and acknowledges situated traditional knowledge of community in nourishment practices, we do not find in Karpagam's narrative a reference to the debates over how a biomedical approach to malnutrition can be a problematic hegemony, especially for countries in the Global South, where land dispossession, colonisation, caste location and socio economic factors shape nutrition. Yet, the accessible language used to present the science makes The Meat of the Matter an important book for people to understand food and nutrition politics in India.

Sayan Deori is a PhD scholar at Tezpur University, Assam.
