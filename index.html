Key Points

  • Anthropic is releasing Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2, two new flagship models built for the most demanding technical tasks. Anthropic says it deliberately prevented the models from gaining capabilities during training that could directly accelerate AI development.
  • Fable 5.2 ranks first with 65 points on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v5, five points ahead of Gemini 4 Carbon and 15 ahead of its predecessor, Fable 5.1. Peak quality averages $26.50 per task, about 2.6 times as much as Opus 5.5.
  • At lower compute tiers, Fable 5.2 still outperforms every other model at every tier with 62 points while consuming far fewer tokens.

Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 reclaim the AI performance lead shortly before the company's IPO, in some cases by a wide margin. Opus 5.5 remains Anthropic's recommended model for everyday use.

Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2 share the same model foundation but, just like their predecessors, differ in access. Fable includes safeguards that block risky tasks in cybersecurity and biology and is available to all users. Mythos removes certain of these restrictions and is open only to organizations that apply through Anthropic's Verification Programs for Cybersecurity or Life Sciences.

Pricing is $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens. On paper, at least, that's a cut from the predecessors, which cost $10 and $50. What users actually pay depends on token consumption, which varies with tasks, settings, and software environments. Anthropic positions the new models for the most demanding technical work and continues to recommend Claude Opus 5.5 for everyday use.

The models produce output up to 30 percent faster than Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1, Anthropic says. An optional fast mode for select organizations speeds up token output by up to a factor of 2.5 but costs twice as much and is available only on certain platforms, including Claude Code, the Claude Platform, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Foundry.

Fable 5.2 jumps to a clear lead on benchmarks

Artificial Analysis’s benchmarks put Fable 5.2 clearly ahead of its rivals. On the current Index v5 at the highest compute tier, it takes first place with 65 points, five ahead of Gemini 4 Carbon (60, High tier), which Google still hasn't released, six ahead of Claude Opus 5.5 (59, Max tier), and a striking 15 ahead of Claude Fable 5.1, which came out only in early September.

Fable 5.2 scores best in five of nine individual tests, according to Artificial Analysis. It hits 80 percent on HLE-Diamond, a test with expert-level academic questions, 73 percent on Terminal-Bench-Science, which tests whether an AI agent can independently run scientific workflows in a terminal, and 49 percent on FrontierCode 2.0, which evaluates whether an agent can deliver useful code changes in real open-source projects.

Ad

Fable 5.2 is weaker in agentic knowledge work and terminal-based coding. It reaches 1,785 Elo on AA-Briefcase, a benchmark for multi-step knowledge work tasks, just behind Claude Sonnet 5.5 (1,813) and within the confidence interval of Opus 5.5 (1,800). On Terminal-Bench 4.0, which measures multi-step terminal tasks in areas like software development and security, it scores 60 percent, tied with Opus 5.5. Kimi K3.1 Pro (67 percent), Gemini 4 Carbon (65 percent), and Sonnet 5.5 (64 percent) rank higher here.

Peak quality comes at a price, though. Per-task costs at the highest tier average $26.50, about 2.6 times as much as Opus 5.5 ($10) and roughly double Fable 5.1 ($12.99). Fable 5.2 still scores 62 points on the Intelligence Index at the lower High and Xhigh tiers, ahead of every other model at any tier according to Artificial Analysis, while consuming far fewer tokens: 98,000 output tokens per task compared to 173,000 for Opus 5.5 at the Max tier.

Mythos 5.2 finds far more security flaws than all predecessors

The new models also set records in cybersecurity, which is seeing intense debate right now. Mythos 5.2 finds more vulnerabilities than earlier models and is better at reproducing and fixing them, Anthropic says. Fable, the publicly available variant, blocks most security-related tasks as a result, including penetration tests and exploit code generation. Opus 4.8 responds to blocked cybersecurity requests instead, and Opus 5 responds to blocked biology requests.

Ad

Anthropic uses an internal test called CVE Rediscovery to check how many published vulnerabilities from the CVE database (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures, a system for cataloging known software flaws) a model can independently find when given the project's state before the fix.

Mythos 5.2 found 10.2 percent more vulnerabilities than Opus 5.5 and 14.4 percent more than Mythos 5.1 in the October 2026 run with 1,829 CVEs, Anthropic says. It led at every compute tier. One security firm reported that one in six confirmed bugs Mythos 5.2 found had been missed by all other frontier models tested, according to Anthropic.

Anthropic throttles capabilities that could accelerate AI development

Anthropic says it deliberately held the model back during training from improving at capabilities that could most directly accelerate AI development, so-called RSI, which is widely discussed as a risk right now. Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 perform roughly on par with Opus 5.5 in areas like optimizing compute kernels for accelerator chips and improving large language model architectures, the company says.

Related capabilities like agentic coding, hardware development, and traditional AI research, including interpretability research, aren't affected, according to Anthropic. Claude now writes over 90 percent of the changes merged into Anthropic's core codebase, under human guidance, the company says. Anthropic also claims the model programmed an operating system from scratch in an internal test.

Claude Fable 5.2 formalizes Fermat's Last Theorem in 30 hours

The example likely to draw the most attention comes once again from mathematics. About 1,000 Fable 5.2 agents fully formalized the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, translating it into the Lean programming language so a computer can verify it step by step, Anthropic says.

Fermat's Last Theorem states that the familiar equation a² + b² = c² has no whole-number solutions for powers higher than squares. Nobody could prove this for centuries until Andrew Wiles presented a proof over a hundred pages long in 1995. Its machine verification has now been completed for the first time. It took 30 hours.

An earlier, unpublished research version of Claude needed 11 days for the same task and relied on a purpose-built environment and guidance from a human researcher. The Fable 5.2 agents started with brief instructions, organized their collaboration on their own, and used no mathematical references, Anthropic says. The only progress metric was a list of intermediate lemmas. The model used one-third of the action tokens of the first attempt and produced a proof seven times shorter.

Anthropic ran Opus 5.5 in the same setup with 1,000 agents for comparison. Opus managed just 26 percent of the formalization in 18 hours and made barely any progress after that through hour 30.

The AI poster boy, the ‘Chinese spy’ and Trump: how an affair may have harmed US security

Aisha Down and Dan Milmo

Reports that the boss of a cornerstone company in the global AI boom has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort are a big deal, given Jensen Huang’s close relationship with the US president.

Aside from Jensen Huang’s dented personal reputation after admitting to having an affair with Alice Huang – an alleged Chinese spy - what is the scale of the damage done to Nvidia and US national security?

The story started to emerge last year when the New York Times and Bloomberg raised questions about a Singapore-based buyer of Nvidia’s powerful chips. There were concerns that Megaspeed was flouting US restrictions on AI chip exports to China by diverting some Nvidia inventory to America’s arch AI rival. Nvidia and Megaspeed denied this.

Then last month came a further startling NYT report that Jensen Huang, a Megaspeed executive, had formed a “close relationship” with Ms Huang and that American officials believed she was a Chinese intelligence asset. Donald Trump had been told, but decided not to act on the information. It is now evident that the relationship was more than just close.

At the heart of the question of what China might have achieved through Alice Huang is the extremely close relationship between the CEO of the world’s second-most-valuable company and the president of the United States.

The New York Times reported on Monday that Nvidia appeared to have successfully leaned on the US commerce department to halt an investigation into potential chip smuggling on the part of Megaspeed. That pressure came through a Trump appointee, who allowed Nvidia to reach out about the case.

Further, Trump has met frequently – at least seven times – with the head of Nvidia since the start of his second term. Huang has flown on Air Force One and taken calls from the president during public events.

Four of their meetings have touched on one of the most sensitive issues in US-China relationships: the controls that the US has put up around the export of the world’s most advanced AI chips, which are almost entirely sold by Nvidia.

The US president first invited Huang to the White House just six days after he was inaugurated, to discuss AI policy and chip exports. The two then met again in July and August of 2025. This was followed by victories for Huang – Trump allowed Nvidia to export previously banned chips to China. Nvidia agreed, in turn, to give the US government 15% of the sales revenue.

Five months later, the tech boss appeared to win another startling concession, days after another meeting with Trump. The president agreed to allow the tech boss to export H200s – a far more powerful chip – to China, in exchange for 25% of the revenue.

It is not unusual for large technology companies to lobby for their preferred policies in Washington; nor is it unusual for Trump to give access – and favourable deals – to men he likes.

But one more thing is now apparent – during this crucial year for his company, Huang was also intimately involved with a reported Chinese spy. The two were first photographed together in mid-2024. Five months after Trump took office is when the two were filmed at the Taipei restaurant. This would have been at the height of Huang’s lobbying push, shortly before Trump agreed to let Nvidia sell the H200s.

What could Alice Huang have gleaned – and passed on – during this affair? Plenty. Glimpses into US policy as it was being made, and to Trump’s own chaotic thinking on AI. And perhaps a world of tips beyond the confines of the White House.

Huang was a key figure for government after government as they formulated their AI policy. He met world leaders from South Korea to Saudi Arabia that year; he joined the then UK prime minister, Keir Starmer, on stage in London.

He would have known the details of the most important tech policy for many governments on Earth – their AI ambitions – because his company was often the only one that could make these ambitions happen. That is certainly true for Britain.

For all the intellectual feats of the systems powered by Nvidia technology, it is Huang’s human intelligence that is most valuable to America’s competitors.

Opinion: Nvidia’s board needs to do some succession planning – sharpish

Nils Pratley

Too big – and too well-connected – to sack? One doesn’t have to be overly cynical to think Jensen Huang, the Nvidia boss, starts with one huge advantage as the chipmaker’s board starts its independent investigation into the alleged honeytrap spy scandal. The world of US big tech is not, to put it mildly, strong on checks and balances on a founder’s power.

Huang does not enjoy the supercharged voting rights that are par for the course elsewhere – his shareholding is a plain vanilla 3.6%. On the other hand, Nvidia’s board does not appear to have spent much time worrying about succession planning and “key person risk” or other standard concerns of corporate governance wonks outside the US.

There isn’t even a Nvidia board member with the title of chair. Instead, a “lead director” is “designated annually by a majority of the independent directors”, which sounds like a formula for ensuring the attention remains wholly focused on Huang, the sole executive member.

It’s the American way, of course: the cult of the superstar founder or chief executive runs deep and there is a fair argument that such set-ups encourage adventurous risk-taking in pursuit of grand corporate visions. But such governance arrangements look unsuited to situations where the behaviour of the boss is under scrutiny and the role of the board itself is being examined.

The investigation will examine whether Huang “has been the target of a foreign intelligence effort” but the independent directors stressed there is now “no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised.”

But legitimate questions don’t end there. Did Huang break Nvidia’s internal code of conduct? Did he mislead the board about his relationship with Alice Huang (no relation), the alleged Chinese spy? Was the board aware of US intelligence reports that Ms Huang had been hired by China’s ministry of state security to spy on the Nvidia boss, as reported by the New York Times? What security precautions were in place? Other boards in other countries might have insisted on suspending the boss until all the facts are in.

In the meantime, the defence of Huang from elsewhere has begun. “An honourable guy. I think he probably has an excuse for it,” said Donald Trump. “Jensen is irreplaceable for America’s AI leadership,” opined Shaun Maguire, a partner at Sequoia Capital, the powerful Silicon Valley investor. Yes, there is little doubt the US tech and political establishment would like this saga to end up in a series of bland conclusions about an everyday infidelity.

Nvidia’s board, however, would be wise not to rely on the “irreplaceable” consensus holding, or the political heat going away. This affair will be trawled over by newly empowered Democrats in Congress because it touches on how forcefully – or not – the administration has been enforcing its own national security restrictions on chip sales to China. This process will go on for months. Huang’s personal relationship with Trump will come under the spotlight like never before.

Will the Nvidia boss, even if he is cleared by the board’s investigation, still look so all-conquering by the end? His lobbying clout in Washington will be diminished. And Nvidia’s shareholders, if they’re doing the job properly, will surely insist on far greater visibility on where the company’s chips end up.

There may come a point where it is in Nvidia’s own interests to move on from the Jensen Huang era. The 63-year-old deserves all his accolades for spotting the enormous AI opportunity for the GPU chips that originated in the world of video games. The rise of Nvidia has been extraordinary. But over-reliance on one individual is also a business risk. Time for some overdue succession planning – it may be needed.

Who is Alice Huang, the woman linked to Nvidia’s CEO?

Rebecca Bailey

There is little public information available about Alice Huang, the woman reportedly at the centre of the Jensen Huang honeypot allegations, a fact that has only added fuel to claims she is a Chinese intelligence asset.

She was the chief executive of a Singapore-based datacentre company reportedly suspected of evading US export controls and routing Nvidia chips to China.

US officials then came to believe she was also a spy for Beijing who was having an affair with Jensen Huang, the chief executive of Nvidia, the chip company critical to US ambitions to stay ahead of China in the AI race.

People briefed on the US intelligence told the New York Times they believed Alice Huang was recruited as part of efforts to learn more about American AI chips, and to try to persuade Nvidia to push for its advanced chips to be made available to China.

China has denied the allegations, calling them “nothing but smears”.

It is unclear exactly when the two Huangs, who are not related, first met.

Alice Huang speaks good English and is well connected within the upper echelons of the global AI chip industry, the New York Times wrote, citing people who had met her.

Megaspeed, the datacentre company for which she was previously listed as chief executive, still describes itself as “a premier business partner of Nvidia” on LinkedIn.

It was in this capacity that the businesswoman was pictured in Jensen Huang’s company several times in 2024 and 2025.

One photo from an industry party in 2024, posted by another attending company on LinkedIn, shows the two sitting next to each other on a couch in Taipei.

The year after, they were filmed together again in Taipei at a dinner hosted by Jensen Huang during Computex, the island’s top tech expo.

Press outside livestreamed footage of the two, seemingly comfortable in each other’s company, with Jensen Huang at one point putting his arm around Alice Huang as she kissed him on the cheek.

The two later left the restaurant in the same car, the footage showed.

A New York-based short-selling firm first raised concerns publicly about their “uncomfortably close relationship” six months ago.

Earlier this year, US officials wrote a memo saying the two Huangs had been romantically involved, the Financial Times reported on Saturday, citing sources who had seen it.

On Sunday the Nvidia chief executive sent a company-wide email apologising for an affair.

He did not name Alice Huang but said he would cooperate with an investigation “regarding the national security concerns raised in news reports”.

Alice Huang’s current whereabouts are unknown, and she has not given a public statement about the allegations.

It is unclear what her current relationship is with Megaspeed, though Culper Research, the short-selling firm, quoted an employee in May as saying that contrary to public reporting, Alice Huang was “actually still our boss”.

The details of her career before that are difficult to ascertain.

Several media outlets have cited a profile matching her name and picture on a Chinese fund database, which listed previous jobs at banks including HSBC and CITIC, as well as roles at Chinese media outlets CCTV and Hunan TV.

However, reporters have been unable to confirm any of the details listed.

Megaspeed is among a number of south-east Asian firms that buy chips to build facilities and then rent their computing power to companies in China.

According to Singapore’s the Straits Times, it was previously known as 7Road International – a Chinese gaming company with ties to state-backed investors.

At the time of the rebrand, it was owned by Huang Le Limited and led by Alice Huang, the newspaper said, before the ownership was transferred to another company in 2023.

Since late 2024, the US government has reportedly been investigating Megaspeed for helping companies in China sidestep American export restrictions.

The New York Times said Singapore questioned Alice Huang during its own investigation into the company, but released her.

Nvidia has previously said it has looked into Megaspeed but found no cause for concern.

The Guardian has contacted Megaspeed for comment.

Indian Economy Prices of most Indian kharif crops rule above MSP as weather impacts production
🇮🇳 Indian Economy • The Hindu BusinessLine

Prices of most kharif crops, barring a few like groundnut, are ruling above the minimum support price (MSP) fixed by the Indian government as the trade fears their production could be lower due to drought and untimely rain.

Agriculture sector stakeholders say they are witnessing record prices for some commodities such as rice and maize. “Prices of even rabi crops such as gram (chana) and wheat are also ruling above MSP,” said Delhi-based exporter Rajesh Jain Paharia.

“Current agricultural produce marketing committee (APMC) yard prices seem to be a natural hedge against climate impact, which has affected the yield. The Centre should consider farmers’ loss due to climate factors before taking any policy decision against inflation,” said S Chandrasekaran, a trade policy analyst.

Higher income for farmers

“Mandi prices are slightly higher than the MSP of various crops due to lower production hit by El Nino,” said Siraj Hussain, former agriculture secretary.

However, Rampal Jat, President, Kisan Mahapanchayat, Jaipur, said: “At an all-India level, market prices for agricultural produce are being reported as higher than the MSP. This is a positive development. However, on the ground, the price for the best variety moong (green gram) is around ₹7,800 per quintal— ₹980 per quintal lower than the MSP. Similarly, for bajra (pearl millet), the price for the best variety is below MSP at ₹2,700 per quintal,” he said. 

Higher than MSP prices mean farmers will get additional income compared to the government-set rates, said D K Pant, Chief Economist and Head of Public Finance, India Ratings & Research.

Chandrasekaran concurred, saying that in some pockets this will “provide rural growth and accelerate the economic engine”. 

Kharif estimate in the works

The rise in prices of kharif crops comes with the Ministry of Agriculture still assessing kharif crop production, taking into account the impact of El Niño-induced drought and recent cyclones in the South.

“The yield of crops has suffered due to climate impact. For example, we were expecting a record 15 lakh tonnes (lt) of urad (black matpe) crop. Untimely rain in Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan. We now expect 8-9 lt of urad crop,” said B Krishnamurthy, managing director of Four P International Pvt Ltd. 

In the case of urad, the crop in Myanmar has also been hit, but Brazil has been of big help. “The demand-supply balance is playing out. Prices have been rising steadily, ruling out any speculation,” he said. 

“In my more than 30 years of career in this (livestock feed) industry, I have never seen these kinds of prices in maize,” Sumit Sureka, Chairman, CLFMA of India, the apex trade body of livestock feed makers. 

Growers hold back cotton

Maize prices have jumped by 30-40 per cent in the past 4-5 weeks on a shortfall in the domestic crop. “The price increase has been unprecedented. We are at historic highs of maize,” said Sureka, adding that feedmakers like him in West Bengal are procuring maize at around Rs 31,000-32,000 per tonne. 

“Basically, the cotton crop is less this year and farmers, who have got low yields, do not want to sell at these prices. They are holding back their produce, anticipating higher prices. Even the ginners and traders are also holding the bales expecting higher prices,” said Ramanuj Das Boob, a sourcing agent in Raichur.

Prevailing domestic prices of cotton are similar to global prices from origins such as Brazil. Further, a steady trend in global prices is seen supporting a firm trend in domestic prices, said Das Boob.

Geopolitics hits imports

“I expect urad and tur (pigeon pea) prices to remain elevated despite zero import duty. Paddy prices may come down as arrivals peak in various states. In case of cotton, the government has allowed import at zero duty up to the end of December. Cotton prices may also fall below MSP,” said Hussain.

However, Jain said geopolitical issues were affecting imports, pushing up freight costs, and this was one of the reasons for prices ruling higher than MSPs.

Sureka said a drastic increase in maize and soyabean prices has led to a sharp rise in cost of production for the poultry sector and is hurting the industry badly. 

Trend to continue

As the arrival of groundnuts is set to begin, a clearer picture of the actual gap between market prices and the MSP will emerge. “In a country as diverse as India, such variations in prices are natural,” said Jat.

Krishnamurthy expected the trend to continue for another 2-3 months in pulses until some alternative emerges.  

Farmers would prefer to sell their crops in the open market rather than selling to the government. “This will reduce pressure on the government to buy pulses and oilseeds at MSPs. There will be some minor impact on food subsidy as procurement is mainly on rice and wheat,” he said, adding that elevated prices will result in food inflation.

Indian Policy Why Dharavi Should Not Be “Redeveloped”
🏛️ Independent Policy & Society • The India Forum

Author: Neeraj Hatekar, Hitesh Potdar, Asim Ali, Tanya Chaudhary, Alaknanda Patel, Nirupama Subramanian, M. S. Sriram, Manish Gupta, Atrayee Choudhury

The “redevelopment” of Dharavi as a modern, upmarket “planned” urban space is legislatively settled. Yet, on the ground, the process is slow and it faces local resistance. We argue that Dharavi has been misconstrued as a “slum”, whereas in reality it is a dynamic and self-organising space of production of labour-intensive goods. Given its complexity, “planned redevelopment” is very likely to destroy its productive energy.

Focusing on Mumbai, we can see how the larger trajectory of the political economy of physical space has created a demand for redevelopment of Dharavi.

In a caste society, the price of labour is closely tied to the place in the caste hierarchy. Labour-intensive work is often the domain of those who are at the very bottom of the caste hierarchy—mainly migrants and the relatively marginalised. Workers continue to be tied to their traditional occupations even when they no longer feel attached to them because of various network and intergenerational learning effects, which make it too costly to opt out.

Also, labour-intensive production requires physical space. This has increasingly become a challenge since the idea of a new “globalised India” has begun to impact urban property markets. Urban property has become increasingly valuable, driven by the combined force of new demand created by globalisation as well as through continued land use regulation and ownership.

This has impacted urban sites of labour-intensive production through pressures for clearance and redevelopment, often by private financial powers backed by the state. In some cases, this is carried out by forcibly taking possession of land that has been occupied by others across generations. Capital accumulation booms because the land is acquired at almost no cost.

Focusing on Mumbai, we can see how the larger trajectory of the political economy of physical space has created a demand for redevelopment of Dharavi. Liberalisation since the 1990s has significantly altered the spatiality of Mumbai. Large-scale industry moved out, releasing significant land parcels for commercial development, especially by the expanding services and finance sectors. The idea of a newly globalised India was central to the exuberance of the country’s property markets during the 1990s.

As Llerena Searle (2019) has documented, Indian real estate firms working with global investors pushed this perception to direct the flow of speculative capital to cities like Mumbai. This, combined with the complex web of regulations governing land use, pushed up land prices in such places. Infrastructure investment moved to centre stage to take advantage of this boom.

Large projects are operationalised to connect high-value corridors, drawing investment into selected sectors and areas. The state remains a deeply committed actor, providing the administrative and political support needed to execute these changes. This includes acquiring land at meagre prices and suppressing agitations by groups that lose their traditional spaces.

The latest Dharavi redevelopment project must be seen in this context. Though there have been several past attempts at “improving” Dharavi, the settlement has remained resilient due to a combination of factors such as multiple governmental jurisdictions working at cross purposes, local political organisation, and lack of adequate funds. Indeed, the economic dynamism of Dharavi as a production site has been amply documented in the literature—for example, Brugman 2013, Saglio-Yatzimirsky 2021.

The clustering of businesses in Dharavi is supported by equally dense networks. These networks rely on labour circulation, subcontracting, skill transmission, and community-based support.

However, in November 2022, the Adani Group won the bid to redevelop Dharavi with an offer of Rs. 5,069 crore. The project is a joint venture with the Government of Maharashtra—which will invest only Rs. 100 crore—and it aims to rebuild housing over a period of 17 years. This time around, the general feeling among businesses and residents of Dharavi is that the settlement will certainly be “broken”, given the political as well as financial heft of the Adani Group. Those running garment and leather businesses have already started to look for alternative sites in areas as far flung as Mumbra and Mira Road, where Dharavi residents have some community networks.

This emerging dispersal indicates a potential fragmentation of the dense labour ecologies that support Dharavi’s productive capacity. This issue is much more than simply a question of relocation.

The clustering of businesses in Dharavi is supported by equally dense networks. These networks rely on labour circulation, subcontracting, skill transmission, and community-based support. The relocation of businesses to outlying areas risks undermining these interdependencies. Ultimately, this moves threatens to disrupt the flexible labour deployment that has historically allowed small-scale production to thrive.

The focus here is on the sector that can claim to have played the driving role in shaping the economy of Dharavi—the leather industry.

Figure 1 shows the location and total number of leather footwear manufacturing units that are registered in various pin codes across the country.

Figure 1: Manufacturing of Leather Footwear in India

Dharavi is nationally a major site of leather footwear production. Only the region around Agra has a greater number of leather footwear manufacturing micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs), as is evident from Figure 1. Though the contiguous belt around Ludhiana and Jalandhar is also a major leather footwear production area, Dharavi leads nationally in manufacturing other leather products such as suitcases, bags, and travel-related goods, as can be seen from Figure 2.

Figure 2: Manufacture of Leather Bags and Travel Goods in India

These patterns indicate that Dharavi is more than a peripheral or residual production site. It is a key node in national circuits of labour-intensive manufacturing. Its prominence across multiple segments of the leather value chain indicates a dense and diverse production ecosystem that goes beyond traditional notions of informal or small-scale industry.

Dharavi’s distinguishing feature goes far beyond its size. It lies in the unique intensity and density of production within a highly constrained spatial environment. This combination of density and diversification is made possible by specific labour organisational models. These models rely heavily on physical proximity, operational flexibility, and embedded social networks.

Immediately after its formation, the Government of Maharashtra passed the Maharashtra Industrial Development Act, 1961, with the objective of providing special provisions for the orderly establishment of industries in designated areas. We compare Dharavi with some of the important industrial areas set up by the Maharashtra Industrial Development Corporation (MIDC).

Dharavi is the exact antithesis of an MIDC project. It is an informal settlement, developed by the labour of marginalised communities with minimal state support.

Dharavi’s economic dynamism emerges from the co-evolution of production systems, spatial arrangements, and community structures, rather than from top-down design.

This contrast becomes analytically clear when looking at how both spaces function. The MIDC areas represent planned industrialisation with a formal infrastructure and regulatory oversight. In contrast, Dharavi represents a form of industrial development that emerges through the organisation of labour, its social networks, and incremental spatial adaptation.

Rather than being an absence of planning, Dharavi reflects an alternative mode of industrial organisation. This model is grounded entirely in everyday practices and constraints.

The first criterion is the total number of establishments in Dharavi against those in some of the major MIDC areas in the state. As can be seen, only the older MIDC areas of Chakan and Waluj have a larger number of establishments than Dharavi. Even the Marol MIDC in the Andheri suburb of Mumbai lags behind Dharavi. This comparison challenges the assumption that large-scale, state-supported industrial estates are more significant as sites of production. Dharavi’s position in terms of the number of establishments indicates that informal agglomerations can rival and, in some cases, exceed formally planned industrial zones in their productive capacity.

Figure 3: Total Number of Establishments in Dharavi and in Some Major MIDC Areas

Density per square kilometre is the next criterion that we use. A higher density implies several agglomeration benefits. It is also an indicator of the efficiency with which land is used. Figure 4 shows where Dharavi ranks in this respect.

Figure 4: Establishment Density Per Square Kilometre in Dharavi and in Some Major MIDC Areas

As can be seen from Figure 4, with nearly 6,000 establishments per square kilometre, Dharavi is second only to the Marol MIDC. Such density reflects an intensive utilisation of both land and labour as well as their spatial characteristics.

High densities enable close coordination between different stages of production, reduce transaction costs, and facilitate the circulation of materials and labour. At the same time, they depend on the embedding of economic activity within residential and social spaces, blurring the distinction between workplace and dwelling.

Next, we compare the share of manufacturing establishments in the total number of establishments across different MIDC areas. Figure 5 shows this.

Figure 5: Share of Manufacturing Establishments in Dharavi and Some Major MIDC Areas

Figure 5 illustrates that Dharavi is a principal manufacturing hub in the state. Dharavi fulfils roles analogous to those of formal industrial estates, despite the absence of the institutional support and infrastructural provisions. If the number of establishments, the density per square kilometre, and the proportion of manufacturing are considered, Dharavi compares favourably to other planned industrial zones in the state, despite lacking state incentives.

The evidence presented here suggests that any intervention that treats Dharavi primarily as a housing problem risks overlooking its role as a major site of labour-intensive production. The reorganisation of space through redevelopment will have far-reaching consequences not only for residents, but also for the broader economic networks that depend on Dharavi’s productive capacity.

Dharavi has been fundamentally misconstrued as a “residential slum”. Dharavi is an organically evolving specialised system, which provides a powerful commercial platform for migrants to overcome their marginalisation. According to scholars like Brugman, Dharavi is a space built by migrants and designed to gain maximum advantage from density and association. It has achieved one of the most advanced practices of urbanism—the design, construction, and evolution of a city-system.

Dharavi has been integrated legally into the global city through its export networks. This integration, however, goes beyond the result of formal planning and involves the cumulative organisation of labour, enterprise, and social networks over time. Dharavi’s economic dynamism emerges from the co-evolution of production systems, spatial arrangements, and community structures, rather than from top-down design.

Dharavi is indeed an organically evolved, complex city system. Nobel laureate F. Von Hayek defines the degree of complexity as the “minimum number of elements of which an instance of the pattern consists in order to exhibit all the characteristic attributes of the class of patterns in question” (1967). As this minimum number increases, the system becomes more complex.

An associated problem is the problem of radical ignorance. Radical ignorance is the situation of being unaware of information that is relevant for making a correct decision not only because the cost of acquiring the information is too high, but also because we are unaware that such information exists. It is a situation of “not knowing that we do not know”.

The challenge of planning Dharavi involves … an acknowledgement that its economic & social structure relies on specific forms of knowledge, labour, & coordination, which cannot be easily formalised or replaced without substantial loss of productivity.

In the context of Dharavi, this complexity appears to be merely a function of the number of elements, but it is in reality dense interconnections between labour, space, and production. Economic activities are embedded within social relations, spatial proximities, and informal institutions, generating forms of coordination that are difficult to observe, quantify, or replicate through formal planning.

Jane Jacobs (1961) popularised the term “organized complexity”, which meant “dealing simultaneously with a sizeable number of factors which are interrelated into an organic whole”. Contemporary understandings of complexity emphasise concrete situations whereby everyday places and things matter and everyday choices are made—where ‘forms of life” evolve through the selection by participants of particular and situated orders from ranges of “adjacent possibilities”.

Complexity theorists in biology and ecology have, for example, suggested that ecosystem and species-level orders are driven by the distributed choices living creatures make for their own prosperity and survival. Echoing this perspective, urban societies and economies like Dharavi may be conceived as emerging and evolving through subjective as well as political choices made at the individual and community levels, which construct larger social and urban orders from different objective possibilities.

In Dharavi, these processes are closely tied to labour practices—decisions about where to locate production, how to organise work, how to mobilise family and community labour, and how to adapt to shifting market conditions. Such everyday decisions generate a form of organised complexity in which production and social reproduction are deeply intertwined.

Economic knowledge about such complex systems is decentralised and fragmented, often also evolving. Such systems evolve in essentially unpredictable and non-linear ways, given the multiple positive and negative feedback loops that are involved. No central planner can have access to the knowledge required for “planning” such a system while preserving and enhancing its life processes.

Significantly, much of this knowledge is embodied in labour itself as in skills, routines, and informal practices that are transmitted through social networks rather than formal institutions. It includes tacit understandings of materials, production techniques, supply chains, and market relations, as well as the ability to navigate constraints of space, regulation, and capital.

The challenge of planning Dharavi involves not only its scale but also an acknowledgement that its economic and social structure relies on specific forms of knowledge, labour, and coordination, which cannot be easily formalised or replaced without substantial loss of productivity. Redevelopment that neglects these embedded systems jeopardises spatial configurations, and also the fundamental processes that generate and maintain value.

A centralised planning authority that lacks a very detailed, context-specific understanding of the socio-economic processes of Dharavi runs the risk of destroying its economic vibrancy, leaving behind an empty shell. For example, deliberate “planning” can potentially impinge on spaces where informal contact between strangers can occur. These seemingly incidental interactions are often critical for economic coordination, enabling the flow of information, materials, and labour across networks of production. Their disruption can therefore have consequences that extend beyond spatial rearrangement to affect the functioning of entire value chains.

The productive processes in Dharavi need to be facilitated. This can be done through better & easier access to credit & provision of basic services such as sanitation, water, good education, & improved law & order.

A living city space like Dharavi fosters economic development and liveliness by promoting diversity of land use and skills, knowledge, and tastes. No single public or private entity can build a living city because it is epistemically and cognitively constrained in trying to construct the essential, self-regulating, and self-refuelling processes that characterise it and must emerge organically within it. These processes include not only visible forms of production but also less recognised activities such as repair, recycling, and the circulation of materials— the forms of labour that operate at the margins of formal economic classification yet remain integral to the functioning of the system as a whole.

The Dharavi Redevelopment Authority is proceeding without the necessary information. We do not have reliable data on Dharavi’s population because the last census was in 2011. Even the 2011 census has been criticised for failing to enumerate shifting populations. The redevelopment report relies on outdated data, sometimes as old as 20 years, to estimate the total number of enterprises in Dharavi.

As an alternative report, prepared collaboratively by a group of concerned citizens and institutions, has pointed out: “Current redevelopment proposals seem to view Dharavi as a greenfield on which fresh structures and thoroughfares are still to come up—ignoring the deeply rooted habitat that already exists. If these proposals are left unchallenged, it could threaten the lives and businesses of many residents” (SPARC et al. 2010). This informational gap reflects a broader difficulty in capturing forms of labour and production that operate outside formal statistical frameworks. Much of Dharavi’s economic activity, such as small-scale manufacturing, repair, and recycling, remains partially invisible in official data, even as it contributes significantly to urban and national economies.

Dharavi should not be “planned”. It is not merely a “residential slum” but an active, self-organising site of production and value creation. Any attempt to ‘redevelop” Dharavi risks extinguishing its economic dynamism. Residents of Dharavi recognise this danger and hence resist.

But this does not mean it should be left alone. The productive processes in Dharavi need to be facilitated. This can be done through better and easier access to credit and provision of basic services such as sanitation, water, good education, and improved law and order. Most importantly, it needs state support against the predations of speculative capital. Such support must recognise that Dharavi is a dense ecosystem of labour-intensive production in which work, dwelling, and social reproduction are deeply intertwined. Interventions that treat these dimensions in isolation risk undermining the very processes they seek to improve.

Recognising spaces like Dharavi as central to economic development requires a fundamental shift in perspective. We must stop viewing them merely as housing problems & start understanding them as complex systems of labour, production, & value creation.

This brings us to the fundamental contradictions of neo-liberal economic thought. On the one hand, globalisation has the potential to create demand for labour-intensive production; on the other hand, it has the potential to usurp the physical spaces where such production takes place.

Since labour-intensive production is often the domain of marginalised communities, the political power to resist such usurpation is also likely to be limited. Moreover, the forms of labour that sustain such production are themselves socially differentiated and embedded, relying on caste, migration-status, community networks, and household-based arrangements. These conditions enable flexibility and low-cost production but also render labour vulnerable to displacement when the spatial foundations of production are disrupted.

The state needs to stand squarely in support of labour-intensive production by protecting the sites where such production takes place from forces of globalisation driven by dispossession. It will need to stand in opposition to global financial capital that seeks to profit from “redeveloping” the physical space that hosts the production. That decision is ultimately a political choice.

Recognising spaces like Dharavi as central—rather than marginal—to economic development requires a fundamental shift in perspective. We must stop viewing them merely as housing problems and start understanding them as complex systems of labour, production, and value creation.

Therefore, any meaningful strategy for labour-intensive industrialisation cannot just focus on markets and exports. It must actively engage with the social and spatial conditions that sustain the workforce itself.
