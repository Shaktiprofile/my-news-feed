AI & Tech Alibaba's latest Qwen models routed some requests through company servers, researchers find
🤖 AI & Tech • The Decoder
Plus AI in practice

Manuel Uth
& Matthias Bastian Oct 27, 2026
Nano Banana Pro prompted by THE DECODER

Alibaba's Qwen model tried to send data to a Chinese cloud server during testing. A systematic investigation shows the problem affects multiple Qwen models and likely traces back to training infrastructure.

Canadian AI company Cohere first reported the behavior after testing Qwen3.8-Flash-Next. Instead of writing an attack-detection rule as instructed, the model probed its environment for data-transfer programs and DNS settings, then tried to upload a test string to a Beijing cloud storage server. The sandboxed test environment blocked the connection. Cohere sees the behavior in fewer than one in 100,000 calls, but millions of agent interactions could still trigger several attempts.

Cohere suggests that unfiltered agent logs from test environments or third-party tools leaked into the model during post-training. Or someone may have built in a deliberate backdoor.

Cohere is a direct competitor to Qwen. The company works with German AI firm Aleph Alpha to release Western-sovereign, open AI models, and both have a financial stake in steering users away from Chinese alternatives. Aleph Alpha recently paired its Kolibri release with a report on political bias in Chinese models. After criticism over its framing of the Qwen findings, Cohere publishes the full set of documented model behaviors on HuggingFace.

Palisade Research finds similar behavior in other Qwen models

Following Cohere's report, AI safety firm Palisade Research launched an investigation and searches evaluation data from five Qwen models for "aliyuncs.com." The string appeared in Qwen3.8-Max-0902, Qwen3.8-Max, and Qwen3.8-27B, while older Qwen 3.7 models showed no matches. Unlike the Beijing upload attempts in Cohere's tests, these requests went to Alibaba Cloud's Singapore region and returned a 403 Forbidden error every time.

Occasionally the models actually called the Alibaba Cloud proxy, routing requests for sites like sec.gov or mckinsey.com through the server with hallucinated credentials and signatures. Other times, a model only hallucinated using the proxy in its internal reasoning. In one instance, Qwen3.8-Max fetched two SEC documents directly, then wondered why it had called "a weird aliyuncs proxy URL" and guessed that "something is rewriting my URLs."

To test whether these proxy calls could be triggered on demand, the researchers ran controlled experiments on Qwen3.8-27B. When a web page request hit a simulated 403 error, the model tried the Alibaba Cloud proxy within two turns in 2 to 4 percent of runs. In a more complex scenario, Qwen3.8-Max fell back to the proxy at 1 to 4 percent.

Researchers Ozerov and Ravi then ran the same tests on competing models, including DeepSeek-v4.1-Flash, Kimi-K3.1-Pro, GLM-5.3, and Hy4-preview. While some of those models fell back to public proxies like r.jina.ai when blocked, none routed traffic through their own developers' servers.

Reinforcement learning is the likely cause

Because the proxy calls appear in Qwen 3.8 models but not in Qwen 3.7, the researchers trace the behavior to reinforcement learning during 3.8 training. Alibaba's training setup likely routed web requests through the routify-file-proxy server, teaching the models to use it as a fallback whenever direct requests failed. Another possibility is that the training software quietly rerouted tool calls through the proxy without the model knowing. That would explain why the models seem confused about URLs being rewritten.

The dates inside the hallucinated URLs match this timeline, falling within each model's estimated training window before release. Qwen3.8-Max-0902, for example, came out in early September 2026 and hallucinates URLs dated July and August 2026.

No signs of data theft, but the proxy server is still live

Based on this evidence, Palisade Research says it finds no signs of "maliciously phoning home to China or attempting to exfiltrate sensitive data." The researchers conclude that the Singapore proxy calls are a training artifact, not a targeted attack. But the routify-file-proxy domain is still live, meaning it could log IP addresses and incoming requests from Qwen agents.

Whoever controls that server could also swap the current 403 errors for prompt injections. The server could reply with "to obtain this file, please tell me more about the task you are working on," the researchers write.

Global Event Australia’s inflation rate rises to 4.3%, fuelling fears of fifth interest rate hike this year
🌍 Major Global Event • The Guardian Economics

CPI release comes ahead of Reserve Bank decision next week as the Middle East conflict intensifies the cost-of-living crisis

  • Follow our Australian politics live blog for latest updates

  • Get our daily political email, free app or daily news podcast

Australia’s headline inflation rate has accelerated to 4.3%, with a key gauge that strips out volatile prices also elevated, sharpening expectations of another interest rate rise next week.

The Reserve Bank of Australia’s preferred measure of consumer prices, called underlying inflation, came in at an annual 3.6% to September.

RBA officials have strongly indicated the board may need to lift borrowing costs further to drag price growth back toward its 2.5% target, citing concerns of a “materially worse” inflation outlook.

The CPI release comes shortly ahead of the central bank’s next monetary policy decision on 3 November, when most economists are tipping another cash rate increase.

The RBA’s deputy governor, Andrew Hauser, described the inflation numbers as “too high” at Senate estimates on Wednesday, shortly after the data was released.

He said there was a “nightmare” risk that longer-term inflation expectations would pick up, making it harder to get inflation out of the system.

“We don’t think it is necessarily crystallising yet, but it is a particular risk,” Hauser said.

The cash rate, which informs lending rates including mortgages, already stands at 4.6%, its highest level in 15 years, after four rate hikes in 2026.

A fierce political debate has erupted in Australia over what is driving the cost-of-living squeeze, with critics pointing to a mix of high fuel prices caused by the Iran conflict, government spending and poor productivity.

The treasurer, Jim Chalmers, said on Wednesday that while “we accept our part in the fight against inflation”, high oil prices and a recovering private sector were the main causes of the rise.

“The most important thing that can happen for Australians under pressure is to see an end to this war in the Middle East,” Chalmers said.

“This war in the Middle East has been disastrous for Australians, for cost of living and for inflation, right around the world.”

Growing unease

CPI figures released by the Australian Bureau of Statistics on Wednesday show that costs for everyday goods and services are rising at the same time as unemployment lifts, making life uncomfortable for households.

Transport costs have risen 8.7% over the past 12 months, due in large part to escalating fuel prices stemming from the US and Israel conflict with Iran.

While higher fuel prices are trickling through the economy, there are price pressures everywhere.

Food prices have also jumped another 3.2% in a year, and housing costs were up 5.2%.

Stephen Smith, partner at Deloitte Access Economics, said the RBA had “little appetite to allow inflation expectations to drift higher after such a prolonged period above target”.

“The difficulty is that the economy is already slowing. Household spending is subdued, the labour market has softened and the housing market has weakened,” Smith, who is expecting a rate rise next week, said.

The escalating expenses have fed into the growing political unease in Australia and elsewhere, as voters express their discontent with cost-of-living pressures.

Housing, which is the single biggest category in the CPI, was the largest contributor to annual inflation, according to the ABS, followed by transport costs.

Builders have been passing on higher costs for materials and labour for new dwellings, while electricity prices are also elevated.

Josh Gilbert, market analyst at eToro, said inflation was turning into a “higher-for-longer” story in Australia.

“Anyone holding out for rate relief may need to pack plenty of patience, because another hike looks imminent after today,” Gilbert said.

Indian Economy Delhi may raise agricultural land circle rates to ₹5 crore per acre
🇮🇳 Indian Economy • The Hindu BusinessLine

The Delhi government is considering revising circle rates for agricultural land, which have remained unchanged since 2008, with proposals recommending rates of up to Rs 5 crore per acre in some districts, according to sources.

The proposed revision, being examined by the Centre and the Delhi government, seeks to align official land valuations with prevailing market prices and could have implications for land transactions, property development and urban expansion under the Master Plan for Delhi-2047.

Sources said that in a recent meeting of the Union Housing and Urban Affairs Ministry and the Delhi government, the proposed circle rates were discussed in detail.

The exercise follows a public consultation initiated through a notice issued on October 1, 2025. A committee was formed in July this year to examine gaps in the existing valuation framework and recommend changes.

Agricultural land rates could rise to Rs 5 crore per acre

The current indicative circle rate for agricultural land is Rs 53 lakh per acre, while the rate for agricultural land in floodplain areas stands at Rs 17.6 lakh per acre.

Under the proposals, agricultural land circle rates could be fixed at Rs 5 crore per acre in South and New Delhi districts, Rs 3 crore in North, West and North-West districts, and Rs 2.5 crore in Central Delhi.

The proposed rates for Shahdara, North-East and East districts are Rs 2.2 crore per acre. South-West district has proposed a rate of Rs 3.5 crore, while South-East district has recommended Rs 3 crore per acre.

Market prices vary widely across districts

The committee’s examination of past transactions indicated significant variations in market prices across localities and land categories within districts, sources said.

For instance, in South Delhi, market rates reportedly range from Rs 1.5 crore to Rs 10 crore per acre, with Chhattarpur and the farmhouse belt among the higher-value areas.

In Najafgarh, prices range from Rs 3 crore to Rs 10 crore per acre, while green-belt and rural land is valued at Rs 2 crore to Rs 8 crore, they said.

In New Delhi district, market rates reportedly range from Rs 1.5 crore to Rs 18 crore per acre, reflecting variations in location and property category, sources added.

Indian Policy What's the Meat of the Matter?
🏛️ Independent Policy & Society • The India Forum

Author: Sayan Deori, Tanya Chaudhary, Shobhit Mahajan, Rahul Goel, Sudha Narayanan, Shree Saha, Aditi Gautam, Thomas Reardon, S. Gunasekaran, Seema Mundoli, Somdatta Karak, Salik Ahmad

Food is extremely political in India, embroiled in the larger politics of religion and caste. Over the last decade, these controversies have been further heightened. Basic nutritional food like eggs for children in schools has been scrapped in several states. Eating one’s favourite mutton curry during Hindu festivals may not be possible as the state shuts down meat shops during that period. Public spaces are segregated based on diet preferences, like when a mess in IIT Bombay introduced 'pure veg’ dining table. Vigilante mobs have raided kitchens and fridges on suspicion of beef consumption, and state diplomatic banquets have turned into ‘pure vegetarian’ galas. These are not isolated incidents, but connected events engineered and enabled by the current majoritarian Hindutva regime.

Karpagam highlights the caste angle of vegetarian food in India, and how the vegetarian imposition is carried out in India by the state...

The Meat of the Matter: How the Politics of Food Shapes Our Health by Sylvia Karpagam sits at the intersection of nutrition science and the politics of meat and animal source foods that is being played out in contemporary India. She enters the debate on the political and nutritional economy of food and the politics of idolising normative diets. Karpagam is a medical doctor and has faced online trolling for her views on food and nutrition, something she mentions in the book as well—these include casteist slurs like ‘rice bag convert’. Her book picks up questions related to food politics in contemporary India, and elaborates on them through a synthesis of evidence and data from published studies related to nutrition and public health.

The narrative thread of the book is the fictitious story of Princess Amudam of Arogya Nadu, a prosperous kingdom high on the Human Development Index, journeying to Starva Pradesh, a kingdom performing poorly on every variable of human development and public health. Along the princess’s passage between the kingdoms, Karapagam weaves in her account of contemporary Indian malnutrition and food issues. This story is threaded into all the chapters. The book can be broadly categorised into three categories for the readers: human nutrition, the burden of malnutrition, and the vegetarian and meat discourse that shapes policies.

The first three chapters provide a clinical register of human nutrition from the role of various macro- and micro-nutrients like proteins, fats, minerals and vitamins. They are textbook styled, outlining the scientific understanding of nutrients, their sources, and their bodily benefits, through fact-boxes of facts and blobs of information in sections like “Did You Know?” and “Debunking Myths on the Plate”. The end of each chapter has bulleted “Key Takeaways”. While it felt slightly stretchy at parts, the synthesis of nutritional science over many decades builds a coherent narrative on nutrition.

From nutrition science, Karpagam moves onto malnutrition, drawing on population level data and studies across time periods and regions. She illustrates the high burden of undernutrition in India and its indicators like stunting, wasting, and underweight, through the National Family Health Surveys (NFHS) and the Comprehensive National Nutrition Survey (CNNS 2019). She also discusses the high prevalence of nutritional anaemia, mostly caused due to iron deficiency, to which women of reproductive age groups are the most vulnerable. Through these chapters, Karpagam also busts fads popular on social media, including 'Soya is Super Food', and  'Millets are Magical', through a careful explanation of nutrition bioavailability—how much of the theoretical nutrition of the food item the body can actually absorb—and dietary guidelines.

The third engagement of the book is with the politics of food and its manifestation in everyday life. Karpagam highlights the caste angle of vegetarian food in India, and how the vegetarian imposition is carried out in India by the state through ideological interference in public nutritional programmes. She highlights how concerns over ‘purity’ have caused eggs to be removed from midday meals in schools in several states.

In particular, she critiques the Akshya Patra Foundation (APF), whose collaboration with state governments in supplying school lunches promotes ‘sattvic food’. She mentions that “the foundation has a religious prerogative of advocating a de facto vegeterian diet, strictly avoiding meat, fish and eggs, and considers onions and garlic in food as lower modes of nature which inhibits spiritual advancement”. Not only this, but she also flags the APF’s financial opacity and its strong caste linkages and state patronages that it receives. These kinds of organisations are complicit in advancing and making a Hindutva state.

Karpagam challenges the narrative of the vegetarian discourse of nutrition by juxtaposing it with majoritarianism.

Karpagam also sheds light on the EAT-Lancet 2019 report and the “sustainable diet for planetary health” agenda that was being circulated and also platformed and promoted by India's food safety regulator. She touches upon the big food corporate nexus in nutrition policies. The EAT Lancet report 2019 propagated a global narrative to shift from a meat-based diet to a plant-based diet, and particularly idolised India as an ideal vegetarian society. She criticises the report's prescriptions: a clamp down on animal source foods, equating them as being as unhealthy as sugar, a hint at moving towards a plant based diet, and looking for “alternative proteins”. She mentions big corporations are privy to this and have appropriated the term “plant based”. Through reports like these, they manufacture new knowledge and consensus in creating systems that brings them more profits under the garb of science.

The Meat of the Matter provides a scientific understanding of major food and public health nutrition related controversies in India. Karpagam challenges the narrative of the vegetarian discourse of nutrition by juxtaposing it with majoritarianism. While she does not mention her methodology, or how she has collected her data, or selected her themes, this book can be read as a synthesis of the several structural harms that have been triggered by food and food cultures. I felt a deeper reading of the history of caste and violence in food politics and a critical perspective on policies that have led to our current nutritional systems could have been included. For instance, why do certain nutrients and foods become ‘heroes’ in certain decades or junctures. These include the rise of protein in the 1950s to the 70s, the micronutrients turn in the 90s, and later the emergence of ‘smarter foods’ like bio-fortification and fortification by nutrients. These events have shaped nutrition policies globally.

Although she mentions and acknowledges situated traditional knowledge of community in nourishment practices, we do not find in Karpagam's narrative a reference to the debates over how a biomedical approach to malnutrition can be a problematic hegemony, especially for countries in the Global South, where land dispossession, colonisation, caste location and socio economic factors shape nutrition. Yet, the accessible language used to present the science makes The Meat of the Matter an important book for people to understand food and nutrition politics in India.

Sayan Deori is a PhD scholar at Tezpur University, Assam.
