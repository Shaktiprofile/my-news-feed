AI & Tech Three fired OpenAI safety researchers say they were never told what they actually leaked
🤖 AI & Cutting-Edge Tech • Daily Headline
Manuel Uth
Oct 24, 2026
Nano Banana Pro prompted by THE DECODER

Key Points

  • Three OpenAI safety researchers who were fired deny sharing confidential information.
  • They suspect their terminations were tied to their efforts to make AI models easier to monitor and warn that the case could discourage employees from raising safety concerns.
  • OpenAI cited alleged policy violations. The researchers say investigators focused on their Signal chats with outside researchers, though OpenAI said Signal was not the reason for the firings.

The three OpenAI safety researchers who were fired over alleged leaks are telling their side of the story. Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni, and Jasmine Wang deny sharing confidential information, question the official reasoning behind their termination, and suspect a connection to their advocacy for more transparent AI systems.

OpenAI dismissed all three after an internal investigation into alleged violations of its policies on handling sensitive information. Each of them had spoken publicly about AI risks before being let go.

In interviews with the Wall Street Journal, the researchers now dispute that account. OpenAI told the Journal that the firings had nothing to do with safety concerns the researchers had raised. But the three say they were never told in writing which information they supposedly mishandled. They also worry the case could discourage independent safety auditors from working with AI companies altogether.

Ad

The Journal's reporting sheds light on what led to the confrontation. OpenAI's security team suspected the three of sharing details about the company's internal model architecture with an external safety researcher and asked them to unlock their personal phones. On those devices, the team found Signal chats with disappearing messages enabled. A week later, all three were fired at the same time. OpenAI said Signal was not an approved communication tool but insisted it was not the reason for the terminations. Korbak counters that disappearing messages are a routine security practice across Silicon Valley.

Ad

A dispute over monitoring AI reasoning steps

The dispute centers on a technical question with major consequences. Should AI companies let outsiders inspect how their models think? Reasoning models can record the intermediate steps of their problem-solving process on an internal scratchpad known as the chain of thought. Companies and safety researchers rely on these traces to understand how models arrive at their decisions.

In a 2025 paper, Balesni, Korbak, and Wang joined leading researchers from several AI labs in calling for continued access to this capability. The issue grew more urgent over the summer, when employees at AI companies began discussing models that think longer but write down fewer intermediate steps. Balesni pushed back internally by sharing a related proposal from Ryan Greenblatt, then chief scientist at Redwood Research.

Ad

Korbak, too, was in regular contact with Greenblatt. The two communicated over Slack and Signal while working together on the analysis of a high-profile incident—the attack by OpenAI agents on Hugging Face—for which Greenblatt had been hired by METR to conduct an independent investigation.

A news report about Astra kicked off the investigation

That cooperation became a liability after The Information reported on September 1 that OpenAI's new model Astra was more capable but revealed less of its reasoning process. OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki dismissed the report as muddled at the time on X, even though he himself had warned of an industry-wide race toward models that can no longer be monitored.

Ad

A few weeks later, OpenAI launched an internal leak investigation, according to the Journal. Balesni says investigators named Greenblatt as the suspected source and demanded to see the Signal chats with him. Greenblatt denies passing confidential information to journalists.

Ad

Wang faced a separate accusation: OpenAI said she had repeatedly accessed an executive's inbox. She says she had asked nearly a year earlier for that access to be revoked, accidentally opened a sensitive email the previous month, did not share its contents, and reported the incident right away. OpenAI says the access itself was not the issue.

The fallout extends well beyond the three of them, according to Korbak. Colleagues are now afraid to talk to outsiders or raise safety concerns internally, he says. In a letter to OpenAI's board, the three researchers call for closer cooperation with external safety organizations.

Separately, a recently published on-site audit raised its own questions about OpenAI's safety practices. Staff from METR and Redwood Research found numerous security gaps in OpenAI's infrastructure during the review, but OpenAI redacted nearly half the final report.

OpenAI also published its own report on a new security architecture that operates without internet access and said it fixed the issues and resumed training in early October. The company says it will announce several new external partnerships soon.

Global Event How does the UK state pension compare with other countries?
🌍 Major Global Financial Event • Daily Headline
ByDharshini David
Deputy economics editor
  • Published
    24 October 2026, 01:09 BST

Getting older is something few of us want to think about. Yet how we fund our later years, and how much we'll get, has been a hot topic.

The triple lock formula - state pensions increasing by the greatest of 2.5%, earnings growth, or inflation - means those benefits have risen far faster than the earnings of workers since its introduction in 2011.

But as housing costs have also been rising fast, pensioner poverty has actually increased slightly in the last decade.

Nevertheless, even if you take those costs into consideration, pensioners are less likely to be in relative poverty - that is, having to get by on less than 60% of the income of the typical household - than families with children or working-age people.

And funding the triple lock has already cost the state billions more than initially envisaged. With that cost set to balloon further, the debate over whether that is sustainable, or fair, has raged for a while.

Andy Burnham intends to start a new system in a few years, which would see the state pension increase by at least inflation or 2.5%, and remove the automatic annual link with average earnings. It will likely mean less generous increases, although it will keep track with earnings growth over the longer term.

But are pensioners being short-changed in other ways? How do we compare with other countries?

One way of measuring that is to look at how generous the typical pension is relative to workers' average earnings - the replacement rate.

The OECD considers the outlook for those born at the start of the millennium in different countries on average wages, and assumes the pension system as it existed in 2022 is in place.

On that basis, looking at the basic state pension, retirement benefits in the UK look low compared with other wealthy countries.

And that is even when you consider that our system compels workers to be automatically enrolled in occupational schemes.

If you consider both of those, then after tax, UK retirement benefits would be 54.2% of average UK earnings, compared with an average of 63.2% across wealthy countries.

But then there is income from other occupational and personal pensions, which are more prevalent in the UK. Include those and the gap narrows.

However, that does also mean that those relying on just the state pension for their incomes are a fair bit worse off than many retired people elsewhere.

So does that make the case for boosting spending on public pensions in the UK?

Well, the amount spent on the state pension is set to top £150bn a year, the single most expensive benefit to fund.

But other figures from the OECD show that the UK state spends a lower amount on total retirement benefits, relative to the size of its economy, than the average wealthy country.

But there are two issues to consider.

Firstly, this is about political choices, and the UK chooses to dedicate larger amounts to items such as universal healthcare.

And secondly, with ageing populations, the OECD reckons the amount needed for pensions may be set to soar in many nations by 2060 (the exceptions largely being those that are deploying changes).

Its predictions for the UK are likely to rise by less under the revised triple lock system being put in place by Andy Burnham but it may still mean more borrowing.

As it is, £1 in every £13 the UK government spends is to pay the interest on existing debt. And countries with more generous pensions, such as Italy, have faced pressure to reform.

So in many ways, with auto-enrolment into workplace pensions, the UK appears to be ahead of the game in insisting on universal privately funded pensions for workers. But that system raises another issue: the gender pension gap.

The gender pension gap is the difference between the average pension income of men and women aged 65 and over, as a percentage of men's average pension income.

It found that the typical gap in most wealthy countries is around 22.8% in favour of men.

But in the UK women typically get a pension that is a third less than what a man gets - in the main, due to the reliance on private pensions - although it should be noted that the most recent data the OECD has for the UK is for 2021.

Women are more likely to have received lower pay over the course of their working lives, taken career breaks or worked part time. And the hit to pension pots can be stark.

So step back and look at the big picture and British pensioners - and those who have yet to retire - may feel others have it better elsewhere.

But the message from all of this isn't to dwell on outrage but to take action.

The state pension has become more generous over the years, but if you want to enjoy the same kind of retirement as those elsewhere, get saving.

That is - if you can, and if you haven't left it too late.

Indian Policy Delhi's Collapsing Buildings are Part of the Plan
🏛️ Independent Indian Policy & Society • Analytical Essay

Author: Tanya Chaudhary, Shobhit Mahajan, Rahul Goel, Sudha Narayanan, Shree Saha, Aditi Gautam, Thomas Reardon, Prithiraj Borah, Rahul Mukherji, Jai Prasad, Jay Prakash Sharma

A recent spate of fires and collapses of buildings in Delhi has brought to the fore concerns around the lack of preparedness for such disasters. In 2026, a building collapsed in Said-ul-Ajaib in April, a fire broke out in a hotel in Hauz Rani in June, and yet another building collapsed in Satya Niketan in September—to cite three widely reported incidents—all of them leading to several lives lost. These are not isolated accidents, but are symptomatic of deep structural failures in urban governance rooted in Delhi’s peculiar history of growth.

Delhi’s infrastructure development, particularly the expansion of the metro, has led to exacerbated rents across neighbourhoods.

Policy and planning documents have often described housing and construction through the binaries of planned and unplanned, formal and informal, legal and illegal, trying to establish a clear distinction between the two categories. Yet, Delhi's forms of informal housing are corollaries of planning, regulation and localised politics. What the policy framework labels as informal is not a deviation from the system but is the system. Poor housing and unplanned residential areas —the sort that become news due to disasters—exist because Delhi’s formal urban housing system, like in most of India, has catastrophically failed to provide affordable housing at scale.

The Delhi Urban Environment and Infrastructure Improvement Project in 2001 categorised Delhi’s housing settlements into eight types. It estimated that ‘planned neighbourhoods’ housed less than a quarter of the city’s population. The 2008–9 Economic Survey of Delhi estimated that one third of the population lived in sub-standard housing—spread across slums, ‘unauthorised’ colonies, old dilapidated areas, and villages, all lacking structural safety and basic services. This count does not include other forms of vulnerable housing such as resettlement colonies, urban villages, and ‘regularised’ unauthorised colonies.

The development of substandard housing can be traced alongside the evolution of Master Plans for Delhi. These Master Plans conceived the urban space of Delhi through regulating land-use zoning, but failed to account for rapid economic and spatial changes. The first Master Plan, from 1962, envisaged a decentralisation of population towards regional centres and spatial planning through land-use zoning. Crucially, it estimated the population to rise to 5 million by 1981, underestimating the inflow of migrants (the plan anticipated 70,000 migrants, while the actual figure came to 2,50,000). Delhi’s population thus grew by approximately 1.5 million more than what was planned for.

Other events between 1961 and the latest Master Plan of 2021 shaped the planning process. The Emergency and the period leading to it in the 1970s saw demolitions and resettlements of bastis, reflecting the state’s approach towards lower-income housing and the micro politics of negotiations of the urban poor to claim their space in the city. The 1982 Asian Games led to a shift in building the city for a global image, rather than a national one. The liberalisation of the Indian economy in the 1990s and the establishment of offices of multinational corporations led to an influx of white-collar employees. These events collectively shifted focus towards developing the National Capital Region, addressing migration and commercial development through high-density, low-rise development, and mixed land-use.

However, the model to develop land remained the same, with the Delhi Development Authority (DDA) having a monopoly on land acquisition and development. The DDA’s housing schemes were too slow to come up, and for the urban poor and lower-middle class, too bureaucratically inaccessible and expensive. Organised private real estate developers had no incentive to build for this segment. Public rental housing barely exists as a policy instrument in India. In response to the growing demand for housing across socio-economic and demographic sections, which the Master Plans never operationalised, developers and others who controlled land produced an informal real estate market in Delhi. The ensuing speculation in land only rose following the Urban Extension Plan of 2001.

The problem of affordability has only worsened since. In recent years, Delhi’s infrastructure development, particularly the expansion of the metro, has led to exacerbated rents across neighbourhoods. New housing stock remains slow to come to the market, and landlords in planned neighbourhoods frequently churn tenants in order to hike rents to the new market rate. The only actors filling the huge gap between demand and supply were in the unregulated private sector, who found two kinds of profitable sites in Delhi to expand with little oversight or penalty: the ‘urban villages’ and the ‘unauthorised colonies’.

Delhi is made up of pre-existing villages that were engulfed by the city's expanding limits. In 1908, a few years before Delhi became the imperial capital of India, the British introduced the lal dora system. Taking its name from the red boundary line in revenue maps that demarcated village habitation areas, the abadi, from agricultural land, the system exempted construction within the lal dora from municipal building regulations and development controls. This continued into independent India. Legally acknowledged as villages, these habitations were exempted from the Delhi Municipal Act (1957) and building bye-laws (1963), under the assumption that these small rural settlements did not need urban regulation. These exempted zones became the 135 ‘urban villages’ of Delhi, enclaves of a past rural society in the urban space.

Delhi’s planned development model was never designed to absorb the speed and scale of migration the city experienced.

Governments did little to revise their assumptions even as the city grew around these urban villages. As demand for affordable housing rose, and the lack was felt acutely amongst migrant populations and the lower classes, urban villages became sites for adding housing. Buildings in these zones did not require floor plans to be sanctioned, or fire and structural safety to be certified. Construction could proceed without any prescribed Floor Area Ratios or setbacks. 1Floor Area Ratio in real estate is the ratio of the total built-up area of a building across all floors to the size of the plot it stands on. Setback is a required distance between the building and street, property boundary. or another property.  No government agency had clear responsibility for these urban villages.

What this translated to was that there were few restrictions on what could be built in the urban villages. Over the decades, places like Hauz Khas, Munirka, Shahpur Jat, Khirki and Said-ul-Ajaib developed dense, vertical, and structurally dubious constructions. Much of the same happened, this time illegally, in Delhi’s 1,797 ‘unauthorised colonies’. These were residential sites which came up on agricultural or other land that owners or property developers had subdivided and sold in violation of the Master Plan.

A third response to the housing crisis was the coming up of bastis and resettlement colonies, starting from the unauthorised occupancy of public or private land and evolving from temporary shacks to more permanent structures. Political negotiations at the local level kept this arrangement going, and when the government did demolish such structures, eligible occupants could be later housed in ‘resettlement colonies’ comprising plots of land or flats. Satya Niketan, the site of the September 2026 tragedy, was a resettlement colony developed in the 1960s and later regularised. The resettlement colony evolved into mixed land use development owing to its proximity to colleges of Delhi University, housing students who did not find a university hostel to stay in. Much of the construction was in violation of applicable laws, yet proceeded without punitive measures.

In all these cases—the exempted urban villages, the unauthorised colonies, and the illegal constructions in resettlement colonies—informal and unplanned buildings were the only affordable housing for groups including students, workers, and international migrants from poor or disturbed regions. The lack of adequate urban planning packed them into multi-storied buildings with no ventilation shafts, common spaces, or fire escapes; the tiny plots opening directly into narrow lanes where brisk commercial activity took place on the ground floors of these buildings. 

While urban villages and unauthorised colonies emerge from two different regimes—one a result of formal exemption and the other of a failure of enforcement—the problems in these two areas bleed into each other. In urban villages, the exemption itself became an invitation for unauthorised construction. While the demand for housing was from the city’s expansion and its rising population, the inhabitants of the urban villages were responding to social changes after their agricultural lands were acquired by the state for developing the expanding city.

These inhabitants, cut off from agriculture, became a rentier class. Most of these urban villages were in prime locations, adjacent to upscale neighbourhoods, which recast aspirations and consumption patterns of the inhabitants, especially of the youth who experienced the city at their doorstep. Driven by an aim to maximise profits from their remaining property, over time, construction went far beyond the lal dora boundary, constructing buildings on commonly held land and adding floors illegally: the origins of the ‘unauthorised colony’. In both cases, political accommodation often followed: unauthorised colonies were ‘regularised ’, and the lal dora boundary was extended.

The problems of housing and disasters like collapses and fires are symptoms of a deeper problem: that Delhi’s planned development model was never designed to absorb the speed and scale of migration the city experienced. Unauthorised colonies and urban villages that filled that gap are products of a planning model that excluded most of the population from formal housing. The accumulated set of assumptions, jurisdictional gaps, and unenforced responsibilities together have created a zone where governance in its current form does not apply.

Recent events have shown the need for neighbourhood-level plans and execution of redevelopment with participatory governance.

Any fix will run into the tangle of property rights, community resistance, and electoral politics. While the 2021 Delhi Master Plan acknowledged the urban villages as a focus area for redevelopment, it failed to lay down a regulatory framework and no effective action was taken on the ground. Building bylaws for urban villages, as recommended by the Tejinder Khanna Committee, never came out. The changes that have come about do not go the full mile. The Delhi Village Development Board was reconstituted in 2025 to advise the government on issues connected with infrastructural development in Delhi's rural and urban villages (Economic Survey of Delhi 2025–2026). It can recommend construction and maintenance of roads, commons, assets, and other public works. Yet it does not yet have a role to check and regulate dilapidated buildings, electric meters, or open wires—hazards which have been fatal in recent incidents.

Delhi's fire and building-collapse crises are not just about poverty or ignorance, but about a deliberately created exemption that nobody ever had the political will to dismantle. When exemptions and lack of enforcement become the rule, the city grows itself. The deviation becomes the system, along with which comes risk. The complete withdrawal of the state from the regulation of the housing market, on grounds that this is outside the purview of the Master Plan, is not a viable policy response. Nor is the demolition of all informal settlements a fair resolution for citizens. The recent events have shown the need for neighbourhood-level plans and execution of redevelopment with participatory governance. This has to go alongside regulation of rentals in planned neighbourhoods and decent social housing for students and young workers who constitute the majority of the floating population of the city. Above all, the failures of the Master Plans to anticipate the pace of change should raise questions about the suitability of perspective planning in Global South cities and prompt us to think of development models that include participatory mechanisms.

Tanya Chaudhary is an assistant professor at the School of Global Affairs, Dr B.R. Ambedkar University Delhi.
