AI & Tech OpenAI launches GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol with lower API prices and some benchmark gains

Key Points

  • OpenAI has released two new models: GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol. They are on par with Anthropic’s best models, but much more affordable.
  • Astra is designed for demanding specialized work in science, law, and finance, while Sol is geared toward quick, everyday tasks. Both are now available to Plus, Pro, Enterprise, and Edu users. Astra’s API pricing is 20 percent lower than that of its predecessor.
  • In the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, GPT-6.2 Astra ranks second, just behind Anthropic’s Claude Fable 5.2, but according to Artificial Analysis, it dominates the Pareto frontier in terms of cost-performance ratio.

OpenAI releases two new language models. GPT-6.2 Astra is aimed at demanding professional work, while GPT-6.2 Sol is built for everyday use. OpenAI says both outperform their predecessors on benchmarks and cost less to run.

GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol are available now for Plus, Pro, Enterprise, and Edu users in ChatGPT Work and Codex. Pro-500 subscribers can use Ultrafast mode with both models. For the first time, Pro-200 subscribers also get Ultrafast access, starting with GPT-6.2 Sol.

Developers can access both models through the OpenAI API. OpenAI says API pricing for GPT-6.2 Astra is 20 percent cheaper than its predecessor's, at $8 per million input tokens, $0.40 for cached inputs, and $40 per million output tokens. GPT-6.2 Sol keeps GPT-6.1 Sol's pricing at $2 for input, $0.10 for cached inputs, and $10 for output per million tokens.

OpenAI calls Astra "the world's best model for scientists" and touts polished slides

OpenAI trained GPT-6.2 Astra to handle professional conventions in fields like law, finance, and accounting more reliably. The company says presentations pull layouts, colors, and typography from templates, matching exact font sizes. Slide titles are meant to capture a slide's main point, while body text stays focused on key details.

Ad

OpenAI's announcement leans hard on the price-to-performance ratio of its models. On Agents' Last Exam, a benchmark that tests multi-step professional workflows, GPT-6.2 Astra scores 5.2 points above its predecessor GPT-6 Astra at less than a third of the cost, according to OpenAI. It leads Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2 by 5.5 points at just 5 percent of the cost. Only Google's Gemini 4 Carbon keeps up with Astra on this benchmark.

On the legal benchmark Harvey LAB-AA, which covers tasks like contract clause analysis, GPT-6.2 Astra more than doubles Claude Fable 5.2's pass rate at about one-sixth of the cost, according to OpenAI. On the internal GDPval-v2 benchmark, which includes consulting, finance, and design tasks, OpenAI says Astra gains 6.2 percentage points over its predecessor while using 22 percent fewer output tokens.

Ad

OpenAI says computer use, already a strong suit of the predecessor, got better again. In testing, the model reportedly built a 3D car model, wrote a physics solver in Python, and animated the results in Blender. In another demo, it modeled a remote-controlled car part by part and produced an animated assembly guide.

OpenAI also claims progress in web design. On the 3D benchmark AA-3D, human judges compare two blinded interactive experiences. GPT-6.2 Astra takes first with 1,318 Elo points, ahead of Claude Fable 5.2 at 1,271 and GPT-6.2 Sol at 1,259.

Ad

On Terminal-Bench-Science, a benchmark that tests scientific workflows in a terminal, the model scores 9 percentage points above GPT-6 Astra, again at less than a third of the cost, according to OpenAI. Astra also sets a new high on the Vals Science Index, which measures how well models interpret biological data and run experiments, OpenAI says.

Ad

Sol nearly matches the original Astra at a fraction of the cost

GPT-6.2 Sol is for users who want a fast, versatile model for everyday tasks, OpenAI says. On the coding benchmark FrontierCode 2.0, which checks whether code changes meet the quality bar of real projects, Sol trails Astra by just 0.5 points at one-fifth the cost. OpenAI says the model also handles non-work tasks well.

GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol have also gotten better at cybersecurity, according to OpenAI. They find and exploit vulnerabilities more effectively, write more secure code, and deliver better bug fixes, with notably higher token efficiency in several tests. Both models hit the "Critical" threshold for cybersecurity in OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, just as GPT-6 Astra did before them.

OpenAI is adding stricter safeguards for potentially risky cybersecurity requests in response. These safeguards should make abuse harder but will also limit legitimate vulnerability research and security testing, OpenAI says.

OpenAI plans to offer the new models through OpenAI Daybreak, which already gives vetted security researchers access to other models with fewer guardrails. The published cybersecurity benchmark numbers come from internal tests with safeguards and classifiers turned off. OpenAI lays out its methodology in the accompanying safety report.

Astra takes second in the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index

In the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, which rolls up nine evaluations across knowledge work, coding, science, and general knowledge, GPT-6.2 Astra finishes second, just behind Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2. Astra jumps five points over GPT-6 Astra, according to Artificial Analysis, passing Google's Gemini 4 Carbon (60 points) and Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 (59 points). GPT-6.2 Sol picks up two points to reach 57, matching GPT-6 Astra's level at only about 15 percent of the cost per task.

OpenAI owns the Pareto frontier on cost versus performance, the line where no other model is both cheaper and better, according to Artificial Analysis. Only Anthropic and Meta models also land on that frontier. OpenAI holds most of the top spots on efficiency too, measured by output tokens per task. Claude Fable 5.2 is the only outside model that shows up there.

Artificial Analysis backs up OpenAI's claims in science and factual knowledge. GPT-6.2 Astra leads on Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1 and places second behind Claude Fable 5.2 on the physics reasoning test CritPt v2, the factual knowledge benchmark AA-Omniscience, and the knowledge benchmark HLE-Diamond. Astra also leads in 3D design (AA-3D) and robotics (AA-Robotics v0.1), scoring 79 on robotics against Sol's 78 and Claude Fable 5.2's 75.

Coding is a different story, though. According to Artificial Analysis, both models have flatlined there. In the Coding Agent Index, measured with the Codex harness at max compute, GPT-6.2 Astra stays right where GPT-6 Astra was. GPT-6.2 Sol actually drops three points from GPT-6.1 Sol. That decline comes from a 14-point slide on SWE-Atlas-QnA-AA, where the model omits key facts from its answers more often than its predecessor did.

Artificial Analysis also flags a high hallucination rate. At maximum effort, GPT-6.2 Astra hallucinates 59 percent of the time, Sol 55 percent. The metric is AA-Omniscience Hallucination Rate, which measures how frequently a model guesses on factual questions instead of saying it doesn't know. These are unusually hard questions, so the numbers don't reflect everyday hallucination rates.

Epoch AI finds Astra on par with Fable 5.2 but weaker on real-world tasks

Research organization Epoch AI tested GPT-6.2 Astra on its own benchmarks before launch. The model scores 174 points in the Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI), tying Anthropic's Fable 5.2. Both models beat what the previous trend for reasoning models would have predicted before Astra, according to Epoch AI.

On real-world tasks pulled from Epoch AI's own research, Astra fell behind Fable 5.2 and barely improved over GPT-6 Astra. According to Epoch AI, the model missed the central question on simpler research projects and spun its wheels trying to fix things. On a task about researching AI data centers, Astra played it too safe and skipped key estimates, something Epoch AI says no contractor could get away with. Epoch AI rated Astra's writing as relatively strong compared to other tested models but still below its own bar.

One thing that stands out is Astra's ability to run complex reasoning within a single output token, with no visible chain of thought. Epoch AI clocks 12.3 serial steps for Astra, up from 10.5 for GPT-6 Astra and 4.2 for Claude Fable 5.1, the strongest non-OpenAI model at the time. Models that can reason this way without showing their work are harder to monitor, a safety concern that already came up with the first Astra model.

On FrontierMath Erdős, a benchmark of unsolved math problems, GPT-6.2 Astra cracked 7 of 68 tasks, each formally verified in the proof assistant Lean. The old record was 3 of 68, set by GPT-6.1 Sol. One of the seven had no known solution before.

Vals AI puts Astra and Sol in the top 10 but finds legal weak spots

GPT-6.2 Astra ranks 7th and GPT-6.2 Sol 9th in the Vals Index, separated by just 1.6 points. The price gap is wide, though. Sol runs $3.18 per task, less than a third of Astra's $10.68, making it the cheapest model in the top 10, according to Vals AI. Astra also got cheaper than its predecessor, scoring 2.6 points above GPT-6 Astra at roughly half of its $21.28 per-task cost. Vals AI says Astra uses the fewest output tokens in the top 10, averaging 91,000 per task.

Vals AI backs OpenAI's claim to the top spot on the Vals Science Index. Astra leads that ranking and takes first on Terminal-Bench Science and VCB 1–100 at about half the cost and half the runtime of GPT-6 Astra. Sol comes in third on Terminal-Bench Science at roughly a quarter of Astra's cost. On the broader Terminal-Bench 4 for agentic coding, Astra places 5th and Sol 7th.

Legal tests are where the models fall short, according to Vals AI. On the HLAB benchmark, Astra and Sol double their predecessors' scores but rank only 11th and 21st. On the Legal Research benchmark, the drop is even steeper, with Astra at 30th and Sol at 52nd. Vals AI says the models make nearly twice as many tool calls as their predecessors but write shorter answers.

In September, OpenAI announced a specialized product called "Astra for Law." It does significantly better than the base model on the Legal Research Bench, reaching 6th place. Vals AI tested both models with a one-million-token context window and 128,000 max output tokens at full reasoning effort.

GPT-6.2 Astra is the first released model to hit OpenAI's "High" threshold for AI self-improvement

According to OpenAI, GPT-6.2 Astra is the first externally released model to reach the "High" threshold for AI self-improvement. OpenAI says the model can meaningfully speed up AI research, on par with giving every OpenAI researcher an extra, highly skilled research engineer.

OpenAI calls this out explicitly as a risk. Models that can help build future AI systems could push the field forward faster than safety measures and human oversight can keep up. The industry is currently wrestling with this under the label "RSI".

As countermeasures, OpenAI points to tighter controls in research environments and its monitoring system Control Room, which can track research tasks and shut them down if needed. The company also says it has expanded monitoring that examines how models reason and act and automatically flags unauthorized behavior.

On alignment, meaning how well the model follows user instructions and communicates openly, the new models match their predecessors, according to OpenAI. In two tests, however, they more often failed to flag obstacles blocking their work, like a missing file or an unavailable search tool.

Global Event UK unemployment unexpectedly eases to 4.6% as government releases Milburn report
🌍 Major Global Event • The Guardian Economics

Wage growth falls to 3.5% in September, as ministers examine how best to get young people into work

  • Milburn urges Burnham to spend billions on young people

  • Who are the Neets? UK youth job crisis in five charts

The UK unemployment rate has unexpectedly fallen as the government released an important report on the youth jobs crisis.

The unemployment rate was 4.6% in September, dipping from 4.7% in August and down from 5% earlier in the year, according to the Office for National Statistics.

However, the number of vacancies fell as employers delayed hiring in response to global economic uncertainty and the use of artificial intelligence, which many analysts expect will lead to widespread job losses.

Business groups said the slump in vacancies posed a problem for the government as responded to Alan Milburn’s report on young people not in employment, education or training (Neets).

Milburn, a former health secretary, unveiled his long-awaited report into youth unemployment on Tuesday morning in London, urging ministers to cut disability and sickness payments for under-25s and invest the money in back-to-work schemes.

The British Chambers of Commerce said it was “alarming” that the number of vacancies had tumbled to its lowest level since 2014 and unemployment for 18- to 24-year-olds was nearly triple the average for UK adults, at 13%.

“This will put further pressure on today’s Milburn review to deliver a sea change in the government’s approach to tackling youth joblessness,” it said.

The number of vacancies dropped to 690,000 in October after soaring to more than 1.3m after the pandemic.

Wage growth also fell to 3.5%, excluding bonuses, in the latest official figures for the three months to the end of September, down from 3.7% in the three months to August.

City economists had expected unemployment to remain steady and wages, excluding bonuses, to fall slightly to 3.6%.

With inflation also at 3.5% and expected to rise for a fourth consecutive month to 3.7% when figures are released on Wednesday, the average worker faces a fall in real wage growth to zero or even negative figures.

Martin Beck, the chief economist at the consultants WPI Strategy, said: “The UK labour market remained relatively resilient in late autumn, but beneath the surface, there were more signs that the economy is struggling to generate jobs. The jobs market isn’t falling off a cliff, but is going nowhere fast.”

The jobs market has proved difficult for analysts and politicians to assess in recent years. It has been called weak after a dramatic slide in the number of vacancies, which illustrates the reticence of companies to hire new staff.

Ministers are known to be concerned that some large employers, including several banks, have said they intend to use AI to reduce their staffing levels.

Economists are divided over whether AI will enhance productivity across all the big sectors of the economy – after signs it has boosted GDP – or lead to improvements in certain industries such as healthcare.

Employer groups have complained that Labour’s increase in employer national insurance contributions, a higher minimum wage and changes to employee rights have increased costs, leading to a fall in the number of advertised vacancies.

The Bank of England increased interest rates earlier this month after a rise in the consumer prices index to 3.5% in September.

It has said that its own surveys showed employers did not expect to increase pay beyond 3.5% to 4% next year, despite the higher level of inflation, and also reported little change looking ahead in their own prices.

However, a growing number of the Bank’s policymakers believe the prospect of high oil and energy prices next year as the Iran war drags on will push inflation towards 5% and force employers to offer higher pay, which will be passed on in higher retail prices.

A spokesperson for the Department for Work and Pensions said the Milburn review would “shape our next steps for young people”. “Despite the difficult global backdrop, the unemployment rate is falling, and the number of people in work is rising,” the spokesperson added.

“We are determined to go further. Our £2.5bn youth employment support will give almost 1 million young people opportunities to earn and learn, while wider reforms including improving jobcentres, will create a more personalised work and skills advice service for all.”

Indian Policy The Architecture of Servitude: Spatial Exclusion in Apartment Housing
🏛️ Independent Policy & Society • The India Forum

Author: Ujjvala Krishna, Rangarajan R., Subhashis Banerjee, Nirupama Subramanian, Asim Ali, Niraja Gopal Jayal, Neeraj Hatekar, Hitesh Potdar

“Servants Not Allowed”. Boldly plastered outside the marble-floored and gleaming lobbies of high-rise apartment housing complexes in many Indian cities, this sign greets anyone who enters. Or at least, all those it targets: the cooks, the cleaners, the delivery personnel, and the nannies who bathe, dress, and care for the children of the households. Turned away right at the elevator door, the domestic worker is governed, categorised, and shunted from their very first step into the new-age urban housing society.

The segregation stems from the typical caste-class anxiety: the assumption that workers will dirty or damage the standard elevators.

This contemporary form of segregation finds its roots in the colonial and feudal history of housing in the subcontinent. During British colonial rule, bungalow compounds achieved segregation horizontally by pushing the worker quarters towards the far edges of the housing plot, ensuring that labour was kept at a distance, yet remained perpetually available. Today, housing developments have insidiously packed this exclusionary logic into high-rise apartment buildings.

By compressing the servant quarter or the maid’s room into a single-room afterthought attached to the main apartment, domestic labour becomes available, governable, and yet, segregated. Here, architecture is not a neutral container. It is an active participant in “othering” the domestic worker, transforming the contemporary home into a site where class and caste segregation is physically structured.

By mapping the everyday spatial journey of a domestic worker across the apartment complex, this essay explores the invisibility of domestic servitude, through the lens of denied dignity of work and spatial justice.

“Domestic servitude confuses and complicates the conceptual divide between family and work, custom and contract, affection and duty, the home and the world precisely because the hierarchical arrangements and emotional registers must co-exist with those of workplace and contract in a capitalist world…. it encompasses and is realised through differences of gender, race, caste, class, and power, in the home.” (S. Qayum & R. Ray, 2007).

Domestic servitude is on the rise in contemporary India, with widening economic disparities. Homes are increasingly dependent on people to wash the dishes, to cook, clean the toilets, walk the dog, take care of the young, polish shoes, and iron clothes. Today, with online platforms and applications available as easy access to labour, companies like ‘Urban Company’, ‘BookmyBai’ and ‘The Maids’ Company’ thrive in large metropolitan cities. They offer deals on services provided by well-trained and efficient domestic workers and assure quality and safety. They offer part-time and full-time services for ayahs, cooks, nannies, and cleaners.

The systems of labour have been compressed and invisibilised, and labour has never been more accessible. But the workers have been systematically planned out of the living spaces and ‘othered’ by discriminatory rules drawn up by housing societies.

From the entry gate of the apartment society, domestic workers are forced to sign in, their names logged in, their bags and belongings checked, and their paths marked toward the entrance lobbies. The enforcement of social hierarchies is built directly into the domestic worker’s movements across the complex. On reaching the entrance lobbies, they are immediately barred from the primary elevators. Relegated to the service lifts left unfinished and lined with plywood, domestic workers often have a long wait in the lobby, even as residents shuttle through the adjacent shinier, quicker, and often safer, resident-only elevators.

Social worthiness is determined and sorting accordingly done. This segregation stems from the typical caste-class anxiety: the assumption that workers will dirty or damage the standard elevators. Such apartment housing treats domestic workers as a unidimensional body of labour but views them as an aesthetic and security risk to maintaining the luxury of the environment.

Upon reaching the apartment, the boundary lines shift inward. Footwear must be left outside to keep the home clean. Personal belongings are restricted to a corner of the kitchen or the storage room to prevent ‘contamination’. Caste marked habits of social distancing are maintained, with domestic workers drinking tea from a designated, separate cup, eating from marked steel plates, sitting strictly on stools and mats on floor. These micro-spatial rules make sure that the domestic worker, even within the intimate space of a home, their place of employment, remains separate and unequal. “A culture of servitude is one in which social relations of domination/ subordination, dependency, and inequality are normalised and permeate both the domestic and public spheres” (R Ray & S. Qayum, 2007).

Servant quarters, however, are often positioned along internal service shafts, or immediately adjacent to garbage chutes and elevator machinery.

An analysis of typical layouts in urban housing across premium and luxury real-estate developments reveals a standardised system of architectural disparity. This is done through multiple ways: one being a massive imbalance in the space allocated to the primary apartment unit, and the attached “servant quarter or maid’s room”. National Building Code (NBC) regulations state that standard residential density calculations should assume an occupant load (OC) of around 4.5 persons per 100 square metres. Often, a servant quarter or maid’s room is 11 to 15 square metres. However, because these spaces are designed as small and secondary service extensions to the main unit, their internal occupant load is severely compromised.

Moreover, the choice of materials serves as a permanent physical marker of social status. The main units feature polished marble, high-end tiles, and wooden finishes, while the servant quarter is stripped of these materials. Flooring is often simple and cheap vitrified tiles, and walls are rarely treated with premium emulsion paints.

The strategic placement of these servant quarters exposes them to significant environmental disadvantages. The main living spaces in the unit face outward to capture natural light and direct ventilation. Servant quarters, however, are often positioned along internal service shafts, or immediately adjacent to garbage chutes and elevator machinery. Moreover, small windows are fitted with translucent glass that block the view and reduce natural light filtering into the space, further reducing ventilation. Even in buildings like the iconic Kanchenjunga Apartments in Mumbai, servant spaces are tucked behind the kitchen with no direct window access to the external façade. But this is not an isolated case; architectural disparity is seen across housing societies in several cities.

These design choices – separate entries, downgraded materials, and strategic placement near garbage chutes – are not accidental. They are tools of social division that ensure the domestic worker remains simultaneously invisible and perpetually available. By spatialising labour, the Indian apartment home becomes a primary site of social division where inequality is constructed.

Instead of building independent affordable housing, developers integrate these spaces directly into luxury apartments.

This architectural script of segregation is not unique to massive high-rise towers. It adapts across different urban scales and settings. In large industrial townships and public-sector colonies, walk-up blocks organise servant quarters on the ground floor, tucked away directly behind the car garages. This ensures that the domestic workers are kept completely separate from the formal residential levels above yet remain close enough for their labour to be available through the day. As Anthony D King writes in his book Urbanism, Colonialism, and the World Economy (1990), “social divisions of labour are architecturally expressed”. It is clear to see the connections between these spatially manifested forms of segregation and division to the socio-cultural ecosystem of servitude and labour. The architecture here maintains the structural hierarchy of the served and the server.

Even in smaller apartment blocks or standalone private developments, where the architectural floor plate is restricted in size, there is a use of micro-architectural separations. By compressing circulation paths, they ensure that the domestic worker can enter, perform labour, and exit through a back gate or service staircase, without ever crossing the primary thresholds of the living areas of the house.

One of the most concerning aspects of this spatial system is how real estate developers exploit local municipal bye-laws. Many regulations require developers to allocate a specific percentage of built-up area to Economically Weaker Section (EWS) housing. Developers frequently label these quarters as the project’s mandatory EWS housing component. Instead of building independent affordable housing, developers integrate these spaces directly into luxury apartments. This allows them to market full-time, on-call domestic labour as a premium amenity for wealthy buyers.

National Building Code regulations and local zoning laws provide minimal oversight for these spaces. In state planning guidelines, like the Gujarat General Development Control Regulations (GDCR), servant quarters are categorised under “ancillary uses” alongside storage sheds and garages. By defining living quarters alongside utilities rather than as habitable spaces, the legal framework also normalises sub-standard living conditions, and those who inhabit these spaces are not privy to the baseline protections around the dignity of habitation.

Contemporary India operates within a globalised, capitalist framework. Yet, it largely relies on feudal labour dynamics to sustain its middle- and upper-class citizens’ lifestyles in urban centres.

Moreover, real estate developers often market and advertise these configurations with “servant quarters” or “maids’ room” as extra amenities, turning spatial segregation into a status symbol. This is further rationalised through interpretations of Vastu Shastra. Websites advise on placing the domestic worker’s room strictly in the southeast corner or the northwest quadrant. They claim that this specific orientation “prevents the servant from usurping the owner’s authority” and ensures that they “remain subservient and under control”. This demonstrates how traditional spatial beliefs are repurposed to justify modern class control and economic dominance.

This systemic denial of spatial justice within the Indian home reflects an embedded socio-economic structure. Contemporary India operates within a globalised, capitalist framework. Yet, it largely relies on feudal labour dynamics to sustain its middle- and upper-class citizens’ lifestyles in urban centres. The residential layout within an Indian home matches this feudal-capitalist structure. Tripti Lahiri in her book Maid in India, writes “we eat first, they later, often out of food portioned out for them; we live in the front, they in the back; we sit on chairs, and they on the floor; we drink from glasses and ceramic plates, and they from ones made of steel and set aside for them.” (Lahiri, 2017).

The core function of a home is to provide safety, privacy, and shelter. However, for the domestic worker living in the attached quarter, the space often comes with constant surveillance. Because these quarters are positioned close to the kitchens and the utility areas, employers can monitor the worker’s movements and interactions. This arrangement complicates the boundaries between work hours and rest. Live-in domestic workers remain permanently on call, meaning that their work hours often extend past legal limits without any extra pay. The physical structuring of the space effectively reduces autonomy and freedom.

Our homes are often not inclusive spaces, but carefully arranged environments designed to dictate who belongs and who does not.

Living in a space or having to inhabit a space that is designed specifically to signify inferiority reduces the dignity of labour. The constant reminders of this through space, materials, segregation through spaces, all systemically reduce the dignity of inhabitation. Domestic workers are forced to navigate environments where their physical presence is considered an eye-sore that must be managed through design. This systematic exclusion chips away at their self-esteem, reinforcing class and caste inequalities.

Zooming out, these choices in the design of a space like a “servant quarter” reflects an ongoing struggle over spatial justice and the right to the city. The domestic worker, a part of the urban poor, provides the essential labour that keeps the city running. However, the city’s pre-dominant architecture consistently denies them a legitimate place to inhabit with dignity. Forcing domestic workers to occupy substandard spaces mirrors the inequalities of our wider urban policies. The domestic worker is given access to luxury enclaves and housing societies only to maintain them, but is structurally and systemically barred from enjoying the spaces they care for.

The architecture of servitude in contemporary Indian apartments and homes is a visible indicator of systemic inequality. Far from being accidental design choices, servant quarters are highly functional tools used to sort, segregate, and reinforce social boundaries. They show that our homes are often not inclusive spaces, but carefully arranged environments designed to dictate who belongs and who does not.

Ujjvala Krishna is a climate and urban researcher at the intersections of people, policy, and the built environment.
